## Lesson 1: The history and evolution of numbers

Chapter: Numbers and Numerals. Prerequisites: none.

**Note** (draft)

> Long ago, people counted with fingers, stones and sticks. A farmer could put one stone in a bag for each goat that went out to graze, then take one out for each goat that came back. Later, people made tally marks and grouped them in fives so they were easy to count.
> 
> As trade grew, people needed symbols for numbers. The Egyptians drew pictures, such as a lotus flower for one thousand. The Babylonians counted in groups of sixty; that is why an hour has 60 minutes. The Romans used letters such as I, V and X.
> 
> The digits we use today, 0 to 9, are called Hindu-Arabic numerals. They came from India, and Arab traders and scholars carried them to other lands. India also gave us zero as a number. With only ten digits we can write any number, however large.

## Lesson 2: Egyptian and Roman numerals

Chapter: Numbers and Numerals. Prerequisites: 1.

**Note** (draft)

> The Egyptians wrote numbers with pictures: a stroke for 1, a heel bone for 10, a coil of rope for 100 and a lotus flower for 1000. To read a number, add the values of all the pictures. So 3 lotus flowers and 2 heel bones make 3020. They had no place value, so they did not need a zero digit to hold empty places.
> 
> The Romans used seven letters: I = 1, V = 5, X = 10, L = 50, C = 100, D = 500 and M = 1000.
> • When a letter is followed by one of equal or smaller value, add: VI = 6, XX = 20.
> • When a smaller letter comes before a bigger one, subtract: IV = 4, IX = 9, XL = 40, XC = 90, CD = 400, CM = 900.
> • Never write the same letter more than three times in a row: write IV, not IIII.
> 
> This year, 2026, is MMXXVI.

## Lesson 5: Elements of ℕ and of ℕ*

Chapter: The set of natural numbers and Number bases. Prerequisites: 3.

**Note** (draft)

> The natural numbers are the numbers we use to count, starting from zero: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, … The set of natural numbers is written ℕ:
> ℕ = {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, …}
> The dots mean the list never ends: there is no biggest natural number.
> 
> ℕ* (read 'N star') is the set of natural numbers without zero:
> ℕ* = {1, 2, 3, 4, …}
> 
> We write ∈ for 'is an element of' and ∉ for 'is not an element of':
> 7 ∈ ℕ, 0 ∈ ℕ, 0 ∉ ℕ*, 2.5 ∉ ℕ, −3 ∉ ℕ.
> 
> Every natural number n has a successor, n + 1. Every natural number except 0 has a predecessor, n − 1. The successor of 99 is 100.
> 
> From 3 to 7 there are 5 natural numbers: 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7. In general, from a to b there are b − a + 1.

## Lesson 6: Cardinal, Ordinal and Nominal numbers

Chapter: The set of natural numbers and Number bases. Prerequisites: 5.

**Note** (draft)

> Numbers do three different jobs.
> • A cardinal number tells us how many: 'There are 12 mangoes in the basket.'
> • An ordinal number tells us the position or order: 'Enow came 3rd in the race.' We write ordinals as 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th … or first, second, third, fourth …
> • A nominal number is a name or label. It does not count anything: a phone number, the number on a taxi, a football shirt number.
> 
> Ask yourself: does it count? Cardinal. Does it give a position? Ordinal. Is it only a label? Nominal.
> 
> Endings of ordinals: numbers ending in 1 take st (21st), ending in 2 take nd (32nd), ending in 3 take rd (43rd), and the others take th (57th). But numbers ending in 11, 12 or 13 always take th: 11th, 12th, 113th.

## Lesson 7: Whole number powers and operations in the set ℕ

Chapter: The set of natural numbers and Number bases. Prerequisites: 5.

**Note** (draft)

> A power is a short way to write a repeated multiplication:
> 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32.
> We read 2⁵ as '2 to the power 5'. The 2 is the base and the 5 is the exponent (index).
> • a² is 'a squared': 7² = 49.
> • a³ is 'a cubed': 4³ = 64.
> Be careful: 2⁵ is not 2 × 5.
> 
> When a calculation has several operations, follow BODMAS: Brackets first, then Orders (powers), then Division and Multiplication from left to right, then Addition and Subtraction from left to right.
> 3 + 2 × 4² = 3 + 2 × 16 = 3 + 32 = 35.
> 
> In ℕ, adding or multiplying two natural numbers always gives a natural number. The order does not matter: 5 + 8 = 8 + 5 and 5 × 8 = 8 × 5. Also 3 × (4 + 5) = 3 × 4 + 3 × 5. But a − b is in ℕ only when a ≥ b.

## Lesson 8: Number bases :Convert numbers in base 10 to other bases and from one base to another

Chapter: The set of natural numbers and Number bases. Prerequisites: 3, 7.

**Note** (draft)

> We usually count in base ten: ten digits, 0 to 9, and each place is worth ten times the place on its right. In base b there are b digits, 0 to b − 1, and each place is worth b times the place on its right. In base 5 the digits are 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4.
> 
> From base ten to base b: divide by b again and again, writing the remainders, until the quotient is 0. Then read the remainders from the bottom to the top.
> 13 in base 2: 13 ÷ 2 = 6 r 1, 6 ÷ 2 = 3 r 0, 3 ÷ 2 = 1 r 1, 1 ÷ 2 = 0 r 1. So 13 = 1101₂.
> 
> From base b to base ten: multiply each digit by its place value and add.
> 1101₂ = 1 × 8 + 1 × 4 + 0 × 2 + 1 × 1 = 13.
> 
> From one base to another: go through base ten.

## Lesson 9: Number bases :Addition and subtraction in other bases

Chapter: The set of natural numbers and Number bases. Prerequisites: 8.

**Note** (draft)

> Adding in base b works like adding in base ten, but you carry when a column reaches b, not ten.
> 
> Example in base 5: 34₅ + 23₅
> • Units: 4 + 3 = 7. Seven is one five and 2 more: write 2, carry 1.
> • Fives: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6, which is one five and 1 more: write 1, carry 1.
> • Write the last carry, 1.
> So 34₅ + 23₅ = 112₅.
>

## Lesson 13: Units of measuring length and distance. - Converting from one unit to another

Chapter: Plane figures: Points and lines. Prerequisites: 3.

**Note** (draft)

> Length tells us how long something is. The distance between two points is the length of the straight segment that joins them.
> 
> The main unit of length is the metre (m). Each unit is ten times bigger than the next one on its right:
> km   hm   dam   m   dm   cm   mm

> 
> Always check your answer: a smaller unit gives a bigger number. A taxi trip of 12 km is 12 000 m.

## Lesson 14: Line segment.- Midpoint property of a line segment and its converse

> 
> Midpoint property: if M is the midpoint of [AB], then M is on [AB] and AM = MB = AB ÷ 2. For example, if AB = 10 cm, then AM = MB = 5 cm.
>

> 
> Be careful: both conditions are needed. A point can be at the same distance from A and from B without being on [AB]. Then it is not the midpoint.
> 
> To find the midpoint with a ruler: measure AB, divide by 2, and mark that length from A.

## Lesson 15: Constructing the perpendicular bisector of a line segment

Chapter: Distance and line segment. Prerequisites: 14.

**Note** (draft)

> The perpendicular bisector of [AB] is the line that cuts [AB] at its midpoint and makes a right angle (90°) with it. 'Perpendicular' means 'at a right angle'; 'bisector' means 'it cuts into two equal parts'.
> 
> Property: every point on the perpendicular bisector of [AB] is at the same distance from A and from B. If P is on it, then PA = PB.
> Converse: if PA = PB, then P is on the perpendicular bisector of [AB].
> 
> To construct it with a ruler and compasses:
> 1. Open the compasses to more than half of AB.
> 2. Put the point on A and draw an arc above and below [AB].
> 3. With the same opening, put the point on B and draw arcs that cut the first two.
> 4. Join the two crossing points with your ruler.
> The line you draw cuts [AB] at its midpoint.

## Lesson 16: Parallel, perpendicular and orthogonal lines. - Notation and properties

Chapter: Distance and line segment. Prerequisites: 11, 15.

**Note** (draft)

> Two lines on a flat surface (a plane) either meet or never meet.
> • Parallel lines never meet, however far you extend them, like the two rails of a railway. We write (d) // (d′).
> • Perpendicular lines meet at a right angle (90°), like the corner of your exercise book. We write (d) ⊥ (d′).
> • Orthogonal also means 'at a right angle'. In a plane, orthogonal lines and perpendicular lines are the same thing.
> 
> Properties of lines in a plane:
> • Through a point not on (d), there is exactly one line parallel to (d) and exactly one line perpendicular to (d).
> • If (d₁) // (d₂) and (d₂) // (d₃), then (d₁) // (d₃).
> • If (d₁) ⊥ (d₃) and (d₂) ⊥ (d₃), then (d₁) // (d₂).
> • If (d₁) // (d₂) and (d₃) ⊥ (d₁), then (d₃) ⊥ (d₂).
