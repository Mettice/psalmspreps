**Summary (the note)**

> A compound is a pure substance made of two or more elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio. Its properties are different from those of its elements: sodium (a soft, dangerous metal) and chlorine (a poisonous gas) join to make sodium chloride, the salt we eat.
> A formula shows which elements are in a compound and how many atoms of each. The small number after a symbol counts the atoms just before it; no number means one.
> • H₂O (water): 2 hydrogen atoms, 1 oxygen atom.
> • CO₂ (carbon dioxide): 1 carbon, 2 oxygen.
> • CaCO₃ (calcium carbonate): 1 calcium, 1 carbon, 3 oxygen.
> A compound can only be split into its elements by a chemical reaction.

## Lesson 1: Introduction to Chemistry

Prerequisites: none · Skill: ch1 (What chemistry is)

**Note** (draft)

> Chemistry is the study of matter: what substances are made of, their properties, and how they change into new substances.
> A chemist asks: What is it made of? How does it behave? What happens when it reacts with something else?
> Some branches of chemistry:
> • Organic chemistry: compounds of carbon (in living things, fuels, plastics).
> • Inorganic chemistry: metals, salts and minerals.
> • Physical chemistry: energy changes and how fast reactions go.
> • Analytical chemistry: finding what a sample contains.
> • Biochemistry: chemical changes inside living things.
> A chemist works safely, measures carefully and records results honestly.

**Note** (draft)

> Each piece of equipment has a job.
> • Test tube: holds small amounts for reactions.
> • Beaker: holds and heats liquids (its marks are rough).
> • Conical flask: holds liquids that are swirled.
> • Measuring cylinder: measures volumes of liquids.
> • Burette: lets out an exact volume, drop by drop.
> • Pipette: transfers one exact volume, such as 25 cm³.
> • Dropper: adds a few drops.

> • Watch glass: holds a little solid or evaporates a few drops.
> Use the right equipment, keep it clean, and handle glass with care.

## Lesson 6: Safety rules observed in the Laboratory

Prerequisites: 5 · Skill: ch6 (Chemistry laboratory safety)

**Note** (draft)

> Chemistry safety rules:
> • Wear safety goggles (and a lab coat) when heating or using acids and alkalis.
> • Never taste anything. To smell a gas, waft it gently towards your nose with your hand.
> • When diluting, add acid slowly to water, never water to acid.
> • Point a test tube you are heating away from yourself and others.
> • Label every container; never put chemicals in drink bottles.
> • Do not return unused chemicals to the stock bottle; ask how to get rid of them.
> • Wash spills on skin with plenty of water and tell the teacher at once.
> • Wash your hands when you finish.

## Lesson 9: Physical and Chemical Changes

Prerequisites: 1 · Skill: ch9 (Physical and chemical changes)

**Note** (draft)

> A physical change makes no new substance. Only the form or state changes, and it is often easy to reverse: ice melting, sugar dissolving, water boiling, cutting paper.
> A chemical change makes one or more new substances. It is usually hard to reverse: firewood burning, iron rusting, cooking an egg, palm wine or milk turning sour.
> Signs of a chemical change:
> • a gas is given off (bubbles, fizzing);
> • the colour changes;
> • heat or light is given out;
> • a new solid appears (a precipitate);
> • a new smell.
> Some physical changes also give bubbles (boiling) or change colour, so look for a new substance.

## Lesson 11: Simple kinetic theory of matter and change of state

Prerequisites: 10 · Skill: ch11 (Kinetic theory of matter)

**Note** (draft)

> The kinetic theory says:
> • All matter is made of tiny particles.
> • The particles are always moving. In a solid they vibrate in fixed places; in a liquid they slide past each other; in a gas they move fast in all directions.
> • When a substance is heated, its particles gain energy and move faster.
> This explains changes of state: when a solid is heated, its particles vibrate so much that they break free (melting); heated more, they escape from the liquid (boiling).
> Diffusion: particles spread out from where there are many to where there are few. The smell of ndolé cooking fills the house; a purple crystal colours still water. Diffusion is fastest in gases and faster when warm.

## Lesson 12: Mixtures and pure substances

Prerequisites: 9 · Skill: ch12 (Mixtures and pure substances)

**Note** (draft)

> A pure substance is made of only one kind of matter. It is either an element (iron, oxygen, gold) or a compound (water, table salt, sugar, carbon dioxide). It has fixed melting and boiling points.
> A mixture contains two or more substances that are not chemically joined. Each keeps its own properties, and they can be separated by physical methods (filtering, evaporating, distilling…). Examples: air, sea water, soil, tap water, palm wine, a soft drink, concrete, milk.
> The amounts in a mixture can vary: tea can be weak or strong. The make-up of a pure substance never varies: water is always hydrogen and oxygen in the same ratio.

## Lesson 15: Methods of separating solid-liquid mixtures

Prerequisites: 13 · Skill: ch15 (Separating solid–liquid mixtures)

**Note** (draft)

> Solid–liquid mixtures:
> • Decantation: let the solid settle, then gently pour off the liquid.
> • Filtration: pour through filter paper in a funnel. The solid left on the paper is the residue; the liquid that passes through is the filtrate.
> • Evaporation: heat a solution until the water has gone and the dissolved solid is left (salt from salt water).
> • Crystallisation: heat the solution until it is strong, then let it cool slowly: crystals form (copper(II) sulfate).
> • Simple distillation: boil the solution, cool the vapour in a condenser and collect the pure liquid (the distillate): pure water from salt water.

## Lesson 16: Methods of separating liquid-liquid mixtures

Prerequisites: 13, 15 · Skill: ch16 (Separating liquid–liquid mixtures)

**Note** (draft)

> Liquid–liquid mixtures:
> • Immiscible liquids (they form two layers, such as palm oil and water, or kerosene and water): use a separating funnel. Let the layers settle, open the tap and run off the bottom layer; close the tap just as the top layer reaches it.
> • Miscible liquids (they mix completely, such as ethanol and water): use fractional distillation. The liquid with the lower boiling point boils off first and is collected; ethanol (about 78 °C) comes over before water (100 °C).
> Fractional distillation is used to get alcohol from fermented palm wine, and in industry to separate crude oil into petrol, kerosene and diesel.

## Lesson 17: Methods of separating liquid-gas mixtures

**Note** (draft)

> Some gases dissolve in liquids: carbon dioxide in a soft drink, oxygen in river water (fish breathe it).
> A gas is less soluble when the liquid is warmer or when the pressure falls, so a dissolved gas can be removed by:
> • heating the liquid gently: bubbles of gas come out (warm water holds less gas);

> This is why a soft drink goes “flat” when it is open and warm, and why fish can struggle in water that is too warm.

## Lesson 21: Differences between mixtures and pure substances

Prerequisites: 12, 20 · Skill: ch21 (Mixtures and compounds compared)

**Note** (draft)

> Mixture or compound?
> • Make-up: a mixture's parts can be in any amounts; a compound's elements are in a fixed ratio.
> • Properties: in a mixture each part keeps its properties; a compound has new properties.
> • Separation: a mixture can be separated by physical methods; a compound only by a chemical reaction.
> • Melting and boiling: a mixture melts or boils over a range; a compound at fixed temperatures.
> • Making it: mixing usually gives no heat or light; forming a compound often gives out (or takes in) heat.
> Example: iron filings and sulfur mixed together are a mixture (a magnet pulls out the iron); heated together they react to form iron sulfide, a compound (a magnet no longer pulls it).

**Worked example** (draft)

> Iron filings and sulfur powder are mixed, then the mixture is heated strongly until it glows. What do you have before and after heating?

1. Before: a magnet pulls out the iron; the yellow sulfur stays. It is a mixture.
2. Heating: the mixture glows: a chemical reaction gives out heat.
3. After: a black solid, iron sulfide; a magnet does not pull it. It is a compound.

**Answer:** Before: a mixture. After: a compound (iron sulfide).
