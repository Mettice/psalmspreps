## Lesson 1: Definition and Evaluation of Financial Possition of a Business

Prerequisites: none · Skill: a2-1 (The financial position of a business: definition and evaluation)

**Note** (draft)

> The financial position (financial situation) of a business is the state of what it owns and what it owes at a given date.
> • What it owns: its resources, called assets (premises, equipment, vehicles, goods, money owed by customers, cash and bank).
> • What it owes: to outsiders (liabilities, such as loans and unpaid suppliers) and to the owner (capital, or owner's equity).
> Evaluating the financial position means listing each asset and each debt and giving it a value in money (FCFA) at that date.
> The financial position changes every time the business makes a transaction, so it is always stated on a particular date: “as at 31 December 2026”.

## Lesson 2: Presentation of financial Position Balance Sheet)

Prerequisites: 1 · Skill: a2-2 (Presenting the financial position: the balance sheet)

**Note** (draft)

> The balance sheet is a table showing the financial position on a given date. It has two sides:
> • Assets (left side): what the business owns. Fixed assets, kept for a long time (land, buildings, equipment, vehicles), come first; then current assets, which change quickly (stock, debtors, bank, cash).
> • Liabilities and owner's equity (right side): where the money came from. Owner's equity (capital) first; then debts: long-term (bank loans) and short-term (creditors).
> The two totals are always equal: that is why it is called a balance sheet.
> Heading: the name of the business, “Balance sheet as at …” (the date). Amounts in FCFA.

## Lesson 3: Definition of Assets, Liabilities and Owner's Equity

Prerequisites: 2 · Skill: a2-3 (Assets, liabilities and owner's equity)

**Note** (draft)

> • Assets are the resources owned by the business that have money value: land, buildings, equipment, furniture, vehicles, stock of goods, debtors (customers who owe money), bank and cash.
> • Liabilities are the debts of the business to outsiders: bank loans, creditors (suppliers not yet paid), unpaid bills.
> • Owner's equity (capital) is the owner's share in the business: what the owner put in, plus profits kept in the business, minus what the owner has taken out (drawings).
> Debtors are assets (they will pay the business); creditors are liabilities (the business will pay them). Do not mix them up!

## Lesson 4: The balance sheet equation

Prerequisites: 3 · Skill: a2-4 (The balance sheet equation)

**Note** (draft)

> The accounting (balance sheet) equation is: Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity.
> It can be turned round:
> • Owner's equity = Assets − Liabilities;
> • Liabilities = Assets − Owner's equity.
> Example: assets 5 000 000 FCFA, liabilities 1 500 000 FCFA, so owner's equity = 3 500 000 FCFA.
> Every transaction changes the equation, but it always stays balanced: buying equipment with cash changes two assets (equipment up, cash down); buying goods on credit raises an asset (stock) and a liability (creditors) by the same amount.

## Lesson 5: Nature of analysis and Notion of Economic Flows and Classification of Economic Flows

Prerequisites: 4 · Skill: a2-5 (Accounting analysis and economic flows)

**Note** (draft)

> A business exchanges with partners (suppliers, customers, employees, the bank, the State). Each exchange is an economic flow: something moving from one partner to another.
> • Real (physical) flows: goods or services (goods delivered, work done, a repair).
> • Financial (monetary) flows: money (cash, cheques, transfers, loans).
> Most transactions have two flows in opposite directions: when a shop buys goods for cash, goods flow in (real) and money flows out (financial).
> Some flows have no counterpart at once (a credit purchase creates a debt; the payment comes later).
> Accounting analysis means studying each transaction to find its flows, the partners involved, and the accounts affected.

## Lesson 7: Accounting analysis of economic flows and the nature and Layout of ledger accounts

Prerequisites: 6 · Skill: a2-7 (The nature and layout of ledger accounts)

**Note** (draft)

> An account is a record of all the changes in one item (Cash, Bank, Equipment, Sales, Capital …). All the accounts together form the ledger.
> The simplest form is the T-account: the name of the account at the top, the left side for debits (Dr) and the right side for credits (Cr).
> Each entry shows the date, the details (the name of the other account, or a short description) and the amount.
> Example: Cash account. Debit side: 1 June, Capital, 500 000. Credit side: 3 June, Rent, 100 000.
> Debit means the left side; credit means the right side. The words do not mean good or bad.

## Lesson 10: The functioning of expense and revenue accounts and Opening, reopening, balancing and closing accounts

Prerequisites: 8 · Skill: a2-10 (Expense and revenue accounts; opening, balancing and closing accounts)

**Note** (draft)

> Expense (charge) accounts record costs: purchases of goods, rent, salaries, electricity, transport. Expenses are debited (they reduce the owner's equity).
> Revenue (income) accounts record earnings: sales, commission received, rent received. Revenues are credited (they increase the owner's equity).
> Opening an account: write its name and the opening balance on the correct side.

## Lesson 11: Single entry recording principle. and Double entry Principle

Prerequisites: 8, 10 · Skill: a2-11 (Single entry and double entry)

**Note** (draft)

> Single entry: each transaction is recorded only once, usually in a cash book. It is simple, but incomplete: it does not show all assets and debts and makes errors hard to find.
> Double entry: every transaction affects at least two accounts; it is recorded on the debit side of one account and on the credit side of another, for the same amount.
> “For every debit there is a corresponding credit.” So, at any time, total debits = total credits in the whole ledger.
> Examples:
> • Cash sale of 60 000: debit Cash 60 000, credit Sales 60 000.
> • Equipment bought by cheque for 400 000: debit Equipment 400 000, credit Bank 400 000.
> Double entry is the system used by OHADA accounting and by businesses everywhere.

## Lesson 12: The Trial Balance and Reciprocity of accounts

Prerequisites: 11 · Skill: a2-12 (The trial balance and the reciprocity of accounts)

**Note** (draft)

> Reciprocity of accounts: because of double entry, every transaction appears in two accounts: as a debit in one and as a credit in the other, for the same amount, each entry naming the other account. The Cash account shows “Sales” on its debit side, and the Sales account shows “Cash” on its credit side.
> The trial balance is a list of all the account balances on a given date, debit balances in one column and credit balances in another.
> If double entry has been done correctly, the two columns have the same total.
> Uses: it checks the arithmetic of the ledger and helps to prepare the balance sheet and the result.
> Limits: it can agree even with some errors (an entry left out completely, or put in the wrong account on the right side).
