## Lesson 1: ; Basic notions of logic

Prerequisites: none · Skill: l3-1 (Basic notions of logic)

**Note** (draft)

> Logic studies how reasons support conclusions. An argument is not merely a quarrel: it contains claims offered as reasons and a claim those reasons are meant to support. Reasons are premises; the claim supported is the conclusion. An inference is the step from premises to conclusion. First identify what a speaker is trying to establish, then ask whether the reasons actually support it. A confident voice, popularity or repetition does not establish that an inference is good.

**Learn: Understand the ideas** (draft)

> Logic studies how reasons support conclusions. An argument is not merely a quarrel: it contains claims offered as reasons and a claim those reasons are meant to support. Reasons are premises; the claim supported is the conclusion. An inference is the step from premises to conclusion. First identify what a speaker is trying to establish, then ask whether the reasons actually support it. A confident voice, popularity or repetition does not establish that an inference is good.

**Learn: Apply the ideas locally** (draft)

> At a Bamenda school, Bih argues that the library should open after lessons because pupils need a quiet place to read. The proposed opening is the conclusion; the need for quiet reading space is a reason. Another pupil might agree with the conclusion while questioning whether staffing makes the plan possible. Separate the proposal from its support before judging it.

**Learn: Reason and check** (draft)

> Underline a proposed conclusion and list the reasons separately. Words such as because and therefore may help, but they are clues rather than guarantees. Ask whether a sentence is giving support or receiving support. A collection of unrelated statements is not automatically an argument.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **argument**: claims offered as reasons for a conclusion

- **conclusion**: the claim the reasons are intended to establish

## Lesson 2: Value of logic

Prerequisites: none · Skill: l3-2 (The value of logic)

**Note** (draft)

> Logic helps us judge reasons, communicate clearly and revise beliefs when evidence changes. It does not make every belief correct or remove the need for factual investigation. Relevant evidence addresses the claim at issue. Consistent claims can hold together without contradiction in the same circumstances. A fallacy is a mistake in reasoning. The value of a conclusion should be assessed through the reasons, rather than through the speaker's status or the popularity of the claim.

**Learn: Understand the ideas** (draft)

> Logic helps us judge reasons, communicate clearly and revise beliefs when evidence changes. It does not make every belief correct or remove the need for factual investigation. Relevant evidence addresses the claim at issue. Consistent claims can hold together without contradiction in the same circumstances. A fallacy is a mistake in reasoning. The value of a conclusion should be assessed through the reasons, rather than through the speaker's status or the popularity of the claim.

**Learn: Apply the ideas locally** (draft)

> A Douala club must choose whether to repair its reading room. A report on the leaking roof is relevant. The colour of the chairperson's shirt is not. The club can ask for evidence without attacking the speaker. If a measurement was wrong, admitting and correcting it improves the discussion rather than showing defeat.

**Learn: Reason and check** (draft)

> Ask what would count as evidence and whether the offered reason concerns that issue. Check whether claims conflict under the same conditions. Challenge an inference politely; do not confuse criticism of a reason with an insult to its author. A good process can still begin with incomplete information, so remain open to correction.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **relevance**: a reason's bearing on the claim under discussion
- **consistency**: claims being able to hold together without contradiction in the same respect
- **evidence**: information used to support or test a factual claim
- **fallacy**: a mistake in the reasoning offered

- A proposal to repair a roof cites water entering through holes.
- The club says its door is open this afternoon and not locked this afternoon.
- A learner checks a rain gauge before reporting rainfall.

Common mistake: “Any interesting fact supports any conclusion.” → “relevance: a reason's bearing on the claim under discussion.”. The evidence must bear on the particular conclusion; interest alone is insufficient.

## Lesson 3: Divisions o Logic

Prerequisites: none · Skill: l3-3 (Divisions of logic)

**Note** (draft)

> A traditional school distinction separates formal logic, which examines the structure of reasoning, from material or applied assessment, which examines its content and truth. **Learn: Understand the ideas** (draft)

## Lesson 4: Types of Logic

Prerequisites: none · Skill: l3-4 (Types of logic and reasoning)

**Note** (draft)

> Deductive reasoning aims at a conclusion guaranteed by its premises when the form is valid. Inductive reasoning offers support of a degree: a larger, more representative sample can improve it without making the conclusion certain. Symbolic logic represents relationships with signs; informal analysis examines arguments in ordinary language. Symbolic and informal are ways of presenting or examining reasoning, not synonyms for deductive and inductive. A claim can be symbolised without becoming true.

**Learn: Understand the ideas** (draft)

> Deductive reasoning aims at a conclusion guaranteed by its premises when the form is valid. **Note** (draft)

## Lesson 7: Significances of laws of thought

Prerequisites: 6 · Skill: l3-7 (The significance of the laws of thought)

**Note** (draft)

> The classical laws help keep reasoning stable and detect incompatible claims. Their use requires care with meaning, time and conditions. ## Lesson 8: Operations of the minds

**Note** (draft)

> In the traditional school account, simple apprehension forms or grasps a concept without yet affirming or denying anything. Judgment affirms or denies a relation and is expressed in a proposition. **Learn: Understand the ideas** (draft)
