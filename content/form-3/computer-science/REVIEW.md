## Lesson 1: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

Prerequisites: none · Skill: s3-1 (Introduction to Artificial Intelligence)

**Note** (draft)

> Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the ability of a computer system to do tasks that normally need human intelligence: recognising speech and images, understanding language, learning from examples, making decisions.
> • Machine learning: the system learns patterns from large amounts of data instead of being given every rule.
> • Narrow (weak) AI does one kind of task (a spam filter, a chess program, a voice assistant); general AI that can do any intellectual task like a person does not yet exist.
> • AI systems need data, algorithms and computing power; they can make mistakes and copy biases in their data.

**Learn: Machines that seem to think** (draft)

> Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the ability of computer systems to do tasks that normally need human intelligence: recognising speech, understanding and translating language, recognising faces and objects, playing games and making decisions.
> A voice assistant on a phone, a translation app and a spam filter all use AI.

**Learn: Learning from data** (draft)

> Most modern AI uses machine learning. Instead of writing every rule, programmers give the system a lot of examples (data) and an algorithm that finds the patterns.
> A spam filter learns from thousands of e-mails marked 'spam' or 'not spam'; then it can sort new e-mails.
> AI needs three things: data, algorithms and computing power.

**Learn: What AI can and cannot do** (draft)

> All AI in use today is narrow: each system does one kind of task. A chess program cannot drive a car. General AI, able to do any task like a person, does not exist yet.
> AI can make mistakes and can copy unfairness hidden in its data, so its results must be checked by people.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **artificial intelligence**: computer systems doing tasks that need human-like intelligence
- **machine learning**: learning patterns from data
- **data**: facts and examples used to train a system
- **narrow AI**: AI designed for one kind of task

- Voice typing on a phone
- Face unlock
- Video recommendations

Common mistake: “Every computer program is AI” → “Only systems that learn or do intelligent tasks are AI”. A calculator follows fixed rules; it does not learn.

**Note** (draft)

> • Health: reading X-rays and scans, predicting disease outbreaks, chatbots giving health information.
> • Agriculture: apps that identify crop diseases from a photo (e.g. cassava or cocoa leaves), weather prediction, drones monitoring fields.
> • Education: tutoring apps that adapt to the learner, automatic translation.
> • Business and banking: fraud detection in mobile money, customer-service chatbots, recommendations.
> • Transport and security: navigation apps, traffic control, face recognition, self-driving vehicles.
> • Daily life: voice assistants, spam filters, smartphone cameras.

**Learn: AI around us** (draft)

> AI is already in many everyday tools: voice typing, phone cameras that sharpen photos, video and music recommendations, spam filters and translation apps.

## Lesson 3: Societal Impact and Challenges of Applied AI

Prerequisites: 2 · Skill: s3-3 (Societal impact and challenges of applied AI)

**Note** (draft)

> Benefits of AI: faster diagnosis, better crop yields, help for disabled people (speech-to-text), saving time on routine work, new jobs (data scientists, AI trainers).
> Challenges:
> • Jobs: some routine jobs may be replaced; workers need new skills.
> • Privacy: AI uses large amounts of personal data, which can be misused.
> • Bias and fairness: AI trained on biased data can treat groups unfairly.
> • Misinformation: AI can create fake images, voices and videos (deepfakes) and convincing false text.
> • Over-dependence and errors: people may trust wrong answers; AI uses a lot of energy.
> Responsible use: protect personal data, check AI outputs, keep humans responsible for important decisions, follow laws and school rules.

**Learn: The good side** (draft)

> AI can save time and lives: it helps doctors detect disease early, farmers protect crops, banks stop fraud, and people with disabilities use phones and computers (speech-to-text, text-to-speech, image description).

## Lesson 4: The use of appropriate Prompts to generate AI responses

Prerequisites: 1 · Skill: s3-4 (Writing appropriate prompts for AI tools)

**Note** (draft)

> A prompt is the instruction or question you give to an AI tool. Better prompts give better answers.
> A good prompt is:
> • clear and specific about the task;
> • gives context (who you are, what level, what for);
> • says the format you want (a list, a table, 100 words, steps);
> • may give an example or a role ('Explain as a Form 3 teacher…').
> Improve by iterating: read the answer, then refine the prompt.
> Never put passwords or private information in a prompt, and always check the answer.

**Learn: What a prompt is** (draft)

> When you use a chatbot or other AI tool, the instruction you type is the prompt. The AI's answer can only be as good as the prompt allows.
> 'Erosion' alone could mean many things. 'List three causes of soil erosion on farms in the North West region, with one way to prevent each' gets a useful answer.

**Learn: Parts of a good prompt** (draft)

> • Task: a clear verb, such as explain, list, compare or summarise.
> • Context: who you are and why ('I am a Form 3 student revising').
> • Format and length: bullet points, a table, 100 words, steps.
> • Role or example: 'Answer as a patient teacher', or show the style you want.
> If the answer is not right, iterate: change the prompt and try again.

**Learn: Safe and honest prompting** (draft)

> • Never type passwords, PINs, ID numbers or other people's private details.
> • Check answers: AI can invent facts.
> • Use AI to learn and practise, not to do your work for you. Handing in AI-written work as your own is dishonest.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **prompt**: the instruction or question given to an AI tool
- **context**: background information that guides the answer
- **iterate**: improve step by step
- **plagiarism**: presenting someone else's (or an AI's) work as your own

- 'Summarise in 50 words'
- 'Explain for Form 3'
- 'Give the answer as a table'

Common mistake: “Typing one word and expecting a perfect answer” → “Write a clear task with context and format”. The AI cannot guess what you need; tell it.

## Lesson 5: Robots and their applications

Prerequisites: 1 · Skill: s3-5 (Robots and their applications)

**Note** (draft)

> A robot is a programmable machine that can sense its surroundings and carry out actions automatically.
> Main parts: sensors (detect light, distance, touch, sound…), a controller/processor running a program, actuators (motors, grippers, wheels) and a power supply.
> Applications: manufacturing (welding, painting cars), medicine (surgical robots), homes (robot vacuum cleaners), agriculture (harvesting, spraying drones), exploration (Mars rovers, deep-sea robots), dangerous work (bomb disposal), warehouses and deliveries.
> Advantages: work non-stop, accurate, do dangerous or boring work. Disadvantages: expensive, need maintenance and power, may replace some jobs, cannot handle every unexpected situation.

**Learn: What makes a robot** (draft)

> A robot is a machine that can be programmed to sense what is around it and act on its own.
> • Sensors detect the surroundings: light, distance, touch, sound, temperature, cameras.
> • A controller (processor) runs a program that decides what to do.
> • Actuators carry out actions: motors, wheels, arms, grippers.
> • A power supply (battery or mains) runs it all.
> Sense → decide → act, again and again.

**Learn: Where robots work** (draft)

> • Industry: welding, painting and assembling cars, packing goods.
> • Medicine: surgical robots, robots that carry medicines in hospitals.
> • Homes: robot vacuum cleaners and lawn mowers.
> • Agriculture: drones that spray crops, harvesting robots.
> • Exploration and danger: Mars rovers, deep-sea robots, bomb-disposal robots, robots inside damaged nuclear plants.

**Learn: For and against** (draft)

> Advantages: they work without rest, very accurately, and can do dangerous, dirty or boring jobs.
> Disadvantages: they are expensive to buy, run and repair; they need power; they may replace some workers; and they can fail in situations their program did not expect.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **robot**: a programmable machine that senses and acts automatically
- **sensor**: a device that detects something in the surroundings
- **actuator**: a part that produces movement
- **controller**: the processor running the robot's program

- Car-factory welding robot
- Robot vacuum cleaner
- Mars rover
- Crop-spraying drone

## Lesson 8: The processor

Prerequisites: none · Skill: s3-8 (The processor (CPU): parts and performance)

**Note** (draft)

> The central processing unit (CPU, processor) carries out the instructions of programs. Main parts:
> • Control unit (CU): fetches instructions, decodes them and directs the other parts.
> • Arithmetic and logic unit (ALU): does calculations and comparisons.
> • Registers: tiny, very fast memory locations inside the CPU (e.g. program counter, accumulator).
> • Cache: small fast memory holding data the CPU uses often.
> Performance depends on: clock speed (in GHz: 1 GHz = 1 000 000 000 cycles per second), the number of cores (each core can process instructions at the same time), and cache size.

**Learn: The brain of the computer** (draft)

> The central processing unit (CPU), or processor, carries out the instructions of every program. It is a chip on the motherboard, usually under a fan and heat sink because it gets hot.
> Its main parts:
> • Control unit (CU): fetches each instruction from memory, decodes it, and directs the rest.
> • Arithmetic and logic unit (ALU): does calculations and comparisons.
> • Registers: tiny stores inside the CPU for the data being worked on now.
> • Cache: small, fast memory close to the CPU for data used often.

**Learn: Clock speed** (draft)

> The CPU works in steps timed by a clock. Clock speed is measured in hertz (cycles per second): 1 GHz (gigahertz) = 1000 MHz = 1 000 000 000 cycles per second.
> A 3 GHz processor has three billion clock cycles each second. A higher clock speed usually means faster processing.

**Learn: Cores and cache** (draft)

> Modern processors have several cores, each able to process instructions at the same time: dual-core (2), quad-core (4), octa-core (8).
> More cores help when running many programs at once. A bigger cache means the CPU waits less for data from RAM.
> So performance depends on clock speed, number of cores and cache size together.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **CPU**: the central processing unit, which carries out instructions
- **ALU**: arithmetic and logic unit
- **control unit**: part that fetches, decodes and directs
- **clock speed**: number of cycles per second, in GHz
- **core**: a processing unit inside the CPU

- 2.4 GHz = 2400 MHz
- Quad-core: 4 cores
- ALU compares two numbers

Common mistake: “The CPU stores all your files” → “Files are kept in secondary storage; the CPU processes instructions”. Registers and cache are tiny; long-term storage is on disks.

## Lesson 9: The processor cycle

Prerequisites: 8 · Skill: s3-9 (The fetch-decode-execute cycle)

**Note** (draft)

> The CPU repeats the fetch-decode-execute cycle billions of times a second:
> • Fetch: the control unit gets the next instruction from main memory (RAM), using the address in the program counter (PC); the PC then increases to point to the next instruction.
> • Decode: the control unit works out what the instruction means.
> • Execute: the instruction is carried out, e.g. the ALU adds two numbers and the result goes into the accumulator, or data is stored in memory.
> Then the cycle starts again with the next instruction. Programs and data are both kept in memory (the stored-program idea).

**Learn: One instruction at a time** (draft)

> A program is a list of instructions stored in main memory (RAM). The CPU carries them out one after another in a repeating cycle: fetch, decode, execute.
> At 3 GHz, a core can repeat this cycle billions of times every second.

**Learn: The three stages** (draft)

> • Fetch: the control unit looks at the program counter (PC), which holds the address of the next instruction, and copies that instruction from RAM into the CPU. The PC then increases by one.
> • Decode: the control unit works out what the instruction means and what is needed.
> • Execute: the instruction is carried out: the ALU may add or compare numbers and put the result in the accumulator, or data may be moved to or from memory.

**Learn: Why it matters** (draft)

> Everything a computer does, from showing a letter on the screen to playing a video, is built from millions of these simple cycles.
> Because programs and data are both stored in memory, the computer can switch from one program to another just by loading different instructions: this is the stored-program idea.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **fetch**: copy the next instruction from memory into the CPU
- **decode**: work out what the instruction means
- **execute**: carry out the instruction
- **program counter**: register holding the address of the next instruction
- **accumulator**: register that holds results from the ALU

- ADD: executed in the ALU
- PC: 100 → 101 after a fetch
- Cycle repeated billions of times a second

Common mistake: “The CPU decodes before fetching” → “The order is fetch, decode, execute”. The CPU must first get the instruction before it can understand it.

**Note** (draft)

**Learn: Memory the CPU uses directly** (draft)

**Learn: Storage units** (draft)

## Lesson 13: Computer ports

Prerequisites: 12 · Skill: s3-13 (Computer ports and connectors)

**Note** (draft)

> A port is a socket where a device is connected to the computer.
> • USB (Universal Serial Bus): keyboards, mice, flash drives, printers, phones; USB-C is small and reversible and can also charge.
> • HDMI: high-definition video and sound to monitors, TVs and projectors. VGA: older analogue video port.
> • Ethernet (RJ45): wired network/internet cable.
> • Audio jacks (3.5 mm): headphones and microphones.
> • Power port: connects the charger or power cable.
> Data transfer speed is measured in bits or bytes per second; transfer time = file size ÷ transfer speed.

**Learn: Ports connect devices** (draft)

> Look at the side of a laptop or the back of a desktop: the sockets are ports. Each type of port is shaped for a certain kind of plug and job, so devices cannot easily be connected wrongly.

**Learn: Common ports** (draft)

> • USB: keyboards, mice, flash drives, printers, phones. USB-C is small, reversible and can carry data, video and power.
> • HDMI: digital video and sound to a monitor, TV or projector. VGA is an older video port still found on some projectors.
> • Ethernet (RJ45): a network cable to a router or switch.
> • Audio jacks (3.5 mm): headphones and microphones.

## Lesson 16: Data capture Technologies 2

**Note** (draft)

> Manual (indirect) data capture: data is collected first (paper forms, questionnaires, interviews), then typed in. A good form has clear questions, boxes for each character, tick boxes where possible and instructions.

**Learn: Collecting data by hand** (draft)

## Lesson 18: Program development life cycle

Prerequisites: none · Skill: s3-18 (The program development life cycle)

**Note** (draft)

> Programs are developed in stages:
> • Analysis: understand the problem and the requirements (inputs, processes, outputs).
> • Design: plan the solution with algorithms, flowcharts or pseudocode, and the user interface.
> • Coding: write the program in a programming language.
> • Testing: run it with normal, boundary and erroneous data to find and fix errors (debugging).
> • Implementation/deployment: install it for users and train them.
> • Maintenance: fix new errors, update and improve it over time.
> Documentation is written throughout (user guide, technical notes).

**Learn: From problem to program** (draft)

> Good programs are not written in one go. Developers follow a cycle of stages.
> • Analysis: understand the problem and what the users need. What are the inputs, processes and outputs?
> • Design: plan the solution as an algorithm, a flowchart or pseudocode, and design the screens.

**Learn: Building and checking** (draft)

> • Coding: write the program in a programming language such as Python.
> • Testing: run it with test data and fix errors (debugging). Use normal data (typical values), boundary data (at the edges, e.g. 0 and 20 for marks out of 20) and erroneous data (values that should be rejected, e.g. 25 or 'abc').

**Learn: After release** (draft)

> • Implementation: install the program, move existing data into it and train the users.
> • Maintenance: fix errors found later, adapt to new needs and improve it.
> Documentation (a user guide and technical notes) is written along the way. When big changes are needed, the cycle starts again with analysis.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **analysis**: understanding the problem and requirements
- **design**: planning the solution
- **testing**: checking the program with test data
- **maintenance**: keeping the program working and up to date
- **boundary data**: values at the edge of the allowed range

- Flowchart drawing: design
- Typing code: coding
- Fixing a bug reported by users: maintenance

Common mistake: “Start by writing code straight away” → “Start with analysis and design”. Without a clear plan, the program may solve the wrong problem.

## Lesson 20: Notions on algorithms

Prerequisites: 19 · Skill: s3-20 (Algorithms: meaning and properties)

**Note** (draft)

> An algorithm is a finite, ordered set of clear steps that solves a problem.
> Properties of a good algorithm:
> • Finite: it ends after a number of steps.
> • Definite (unambiguous): each step has one clear meaning.
> • Ordered: steps are in the right sequence.
> • Has inputs and outputs.
> • Effective: each step can actually be done.
> Everyday examples: a recipe, directions to a friend's house, the steps to make a mobile-money transfer.
> Tracing an algorithm means following it step by step with values to check what it does.

**Learn: What an algorithm is** (draft)

> An algorithm is a finite, ordered set of clear steps that solves a problem. Computers run algorithms written as programs, but people use algorithms every day too: a recipe for puff-puff, directions to a friend's house, the steps for a mobile-money transfer.

**Learn: A good algorithm is…** (draft)

> • Finite: it stops after a number of steps.
> • Definite: every step is clear. 'Add some salt' is vague; 'add 5 g of salt' is definite.
> • Ordered: the steps come in the correct sequence.
> • It takes inputs and produces outputs.
> • Effective: each step can actually be carried out.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **ambiguous**: having more than one possible meaning

- Trace: 10 → 15 → 20 → 25
- Swap two variables using a sum

## Lesson 21: Algorithmic instructions

Prerequisites: 20 · Skill: s3-21 (Algorithmic instructions: input, output, assignment, selection and iteration)

**Note** (draft)

> Basic instructions used in pseudocode:
> • INPUT / READ: get data. OUTPUT / PRINT: show results.
> • Assignment: store a value in a variable, e.g. total ← total + mark.
> • Sequence: steps carried out one after another.
> • Selection: IF condition THEN … ELSE … ENDIF chooses between paths.
> • Iteration (loops): FOR i ← 1 TO n … NEXT i (a fixed number of times); WHILE condition DO … ENDWHILE (while a condition is true); REPEAT … UNTIL condition.
> Comparison operators: =, <>, <, >, <=, >=.

**Learn: The building blocks** (draft)

> Every algorithm is made from a few kinds of instruction:
> • Input (INPUT, READ): get data, e.g. INPUT mark.
> • Output (OUTPUT, PRINT): show results.
> • Assignment: store a value in a variable, e.g. total ← total + mark (read the arrow as 'becomes').
> • Sequence: carry out steps in order, one after another.

**Learn: Selection** (draft)

> Selection lets an algorithm choose. IF condition THEN … ELSE … ENDIF.
> IF mark >= 10 THEN OUTPUT 'Pass' ELSE OUTPUT 'Fail' ENDIF.
> Conditions use comparison operators: = (equal), <> (not equal), <, >, <=, >=. Only one branch runs.

**Learn: Iteration (loops)** (draft)

> Loops repeat steps:
> • FOR i ← 1 TO 5 … NEXT i: repeats a fixed number of times (5).
> • WHILE condition DO … ENDWHILE: repeats while the condition is true (it may run zero times).
> • REPEAT … UNTIL condition: runs at least once, until the condition becomes true.
> total ← 0; FOR i ← 1 TO 4: total ← total + i gives 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **assignment**: storing a value in a variable
- **selection**: choosing between paths with IF
- **iteration**: repeating steps in a loop
- **condition**: a test that is true or false

- IF x > 0 THEN OUTPUT 'positive'
- FOR i ← 1 TO 3: OUTPUT i
- count ← count + 1

Common mistake: “FOR i ← 2 TO 5 runs 3 times” → “It runs 4 times: 2, 3, 4, 5”. Count both the first and last values: last − first + 1.

## Lesson 22: Flowcharts

Prerequisites: 21 · Skill: s3-22 (Flowcharts)

**Note** (draft)

> A flowchart shows an algorithm as a diagram. Standard symbols:
> • Oval (terminal): Start and Stop.
> • Parallelogram: input or output.
> • Rectangle: process (calculation, assignment).
> • Diamond: decision (a yes/no question), with two exits.
> • Arrows (flow lines): show the order of steps.
> A loop is drawn as an arrow going back to an earlier step. Every flowchart has one Start and at least one Stop.

**Learn: Algorithms as diagrams** (draft)

> A flowchart shows the steps of an algorithm with standard shapes joined by arrows. It makes the order of steps and the decisions easy to see.
> • Oval (terminal): Start and Stop.
> • Parallelogram: input or output (Read mark, Print result).
> • Rectangle: process (total ← total + mark).
> • Diamond: decision, a yes/no question with two exits.
> • Arrows (flow lines): the order to follow.

**Learn: Decisions and loops** (draft)

> A decision diamond asks a question such as 'Is mark ≥ 10?'. One arrow leaves for Yes and another for No.
> A loop is made by an arrow that goes back to an earlier step: for example, 'Is the PIN correct?' No → back to 'Input PIN'.
> Make sure every loop can end, or the algorithm will run forever.

**Learn: Reading a flowchart** (draft)

> Follow the arrows from Start with the given values, writing down each variable as it changes, until you reach Stop.
> Start → Input L, W → A ← L × W → Output A → Stop. With L = 6 and W = 4, the output is 24.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **flowchart**: a diagram of an algorithm using standard symbols
- **terminal**: Start or Stop (oval)
- **decision**: a yes/no question (diamond)
- **process**: a calculation or assignment (rectangle)
- **flow line**: an arrow showing the order of steps

- Diamond: 'Is age ≥ 18?'
- Parallelogram: 'Input name'
- Rectangle: 'A ← L × W'

Common mistake: “Using a rectangle for a yes/no question” → “Use a diamond for every decision”. Diamonds have two exits for the two answers.

## Lesson 28: Protection of intellectual property

**Note** (draft)

**Learn: Ideas can be owned** (draft)

## Lesson 29: Digital rights management

Prerequisites: 28 · Skill: s3-29 (Digital rights management (DRM))

**Note** (draft)

> Digital rights management (DRM) is technology that controls how digital content (music, films, e-books, software, games) can be used, to protect copyright.
> Examples:
> • Licence keys and product activation for software.
> • Streaming services that let you watch but not download, or only on registered devices.
> • E-books that cannot be copied or printed; encrypted DVDs; region codes.
> • Watermarks that identify the owner.
> Advantages: protects creators' income, reduces piracy. Disadvantages: can stop fair uses, may need internet to check licences, content may become unusable if the service closes.

**Learn: Protecting digital content** (draft)

> Digital files can be copied perfectly in seconds. Digital rights management (DRM) is technology that controls how music, films, e-books, games and software may be used, so copyright holders are paid.

**Learn: How DRM works** (draft)

> • Licence keys and activation: a code proves you bought the software.
> • Streaming limits: watch or listen online, often without downloading, on a limited number of devices.
> • Copy and print restrictions on e-books; encryption on DVDs; region codes.
> • Watermarks: visible or hidden marks showing who owns or bought the content.

**Learn: For and against** (draft)

> For: it reduces piracy and protects the income of musicians, film makers and programmers.
> Against: it can stop honest uses (moving a song you bought to a new phone), may need the internet to check licences, and content can become unusable if the service closes.
> Avoid 'cracked' software that removes DRM: it is illegal and often carries malware.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **DRM**: digital rights management: technology controlling use of digital content
- **licence key**: a code that activates paid software
- **watermark**: a mark showing ownership
- **encryption**: scrambling data so only authorised users can read it

- Streaming film: watch only
- Software activation key
- E-book: no copying

Common mistake: “DRM makes content free to copy” → “DRM controls and limits copying”. Its purpose is to protect copyright.

## Lesson 30: Creative Common licenses

Prerequisites: 28 · Skill: s3-30 (Creative Commons licences)

**Note** (draft)

> Creative Commons (CC) licences let creators share their work while keeping some rights. Others may use it if they follow the conditions:
> • BY (Attribution): give credit to the creator. • SA (ShareAlike): share new works under the same licence.
> • NC (NonCommercial): no use to make money. • ND (NoDerivatives): no changes; share only as it is.
> Licences combine these, e.g. CC BY-SA, CC BY-NC-ND. CC0 means 'no rights reserved' (public domain).
> Many images on Wikipedia and many free educational resources use CC licences.

**Learn: Sharing with conditions** (draft)

> Normally, copyright means 'all rights reserved': you need permission to reuse a work. Creative Commons (CC) licences let creators say in advance 'you may use this, if…'.
> This makes it easy for teachers, students and artists to share and reuse legally.

**Learn: The building blocks** (draft)

> • BY (Attribution): give credit to the creator.
> • SA (ShareAlike): if you adapt it, share your version under the same licence.
> • NC (NonCommercial): do not use it to make money.
> • ND (NoDerivatives): share it only as it is; no changes.
> They combine: CC BY, CC BY-SA, CC BY-NC, CC BY-NC-SA, CC BY-ND, CC BY-NC-ND (the strictest).

**Learn: Public domain and practice** (draft)

> CC0 means 'no rights reserved': the creator gives the work to the public domain for any use.
> When you use a CC work, write the title, creator, source and licence, e.g. 'Photo: Mount Cameroon by A. Tabe, CC BY-SA, Wikimedia Commons'.
> Check the conditions before changing a work or using it for business.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **Creative Commons**: licences that let creators share work with conditions
- **attribution**: giving credit to the creator
- **non-commercial**: not used to make money
- **public domain**: free for anyone to use

- CC BY: any use with credit
- CC BY-NC: not for sale
- CC0: no rights reserved

Common mistake: “A CC licence means you can do anything without credit” → “Most CC licences require credit (BY) and may forbid changes or sale”. Always read the letters of the licence.

## Lesson 32: Off-the-shelf and Bespoke software

Prerequisites: 26 · Skill: s3-32 (Off-the-shelf and bespoke (custom) software)

**Note** (draft)

> • Off-the-shelf (packaged) software is ready-made for many users (word processors, accounting packages, antivirus). Advantages: cheaper, available at once, well tested, lots of help and training. Disadvantages: may have features you do not need or lack ones you do; you cannot change it.
> • Bespoke (custom-written) software is made for one organisation's exact needs (a hospital's patient system, a bank's mobile-money platform). Advantages: fits exactly, can be changed, can give a business advantage. Disadvantages: expensive, takes time to develop, may have more bugs at first, depends on the developers for support.
> Choose by cost, time, how special the needs are, and support.

**Learn: Ready-made software** (draft)

> Off-the-shelf (packaged) software is written once and sold or given to many users: word processors, spreadsheets, antivirus, accounting packages.
> Advantages: cheaper (the cost is shared), available at once, well tested, with plenty of manuals, videos and help.
> Disadvantages: it may not do exactly what you need, may have many features you never use, and you cannot change its code.

**Learn: Made to measure** (draft)

> Bespoke (custom-written) software is made for one organisation's exact needs: a hospital's patient system, a university's results system, a mobile-money platform.
> Advantages: it fits the way the organisation works, can be changed later, and can give an advantage over competitors.
> Disadvantages: expensive, slow to develop, may have bugs at first, and depends on the developers for support.

**Learn: Choosing** (draft)

> Ask: Are our needs common or special? How much money and time do we have? Who will support it?
> Common needs and small budgets → off-the-shelf. Unique, large-scale needs → bespoke, or customised off-the-shelf software as a middle way.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **off-the-shelf software**: ready-made software sold to many users
- **bespoke software**: software written for one organisation
- **customised software**: packaged software adjusted for one user's needs

- LibreOffice: off-the-shelf
- Hospital records system: bespoke
- Accounting package with add-ons: customised

Common mistake: “Bespoke software is always the best choice” → “It is best only when needs are special and the budget allows”. For common needs, off-the-shelf software is cheaper, faster and well tested.
