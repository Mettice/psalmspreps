## Lesson 1: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

Prerequisites: none · Skill: s3-1 (Introduction to Artificial Intelligence)

**Note** (draft)

> Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the ability of a computer system to do tasks that normally need human intelligence: recognising speech and images, understanding language, learning from examples, making decisions.
> • Machine learning: the system learns patterns from large amounts of data instead of being given every rule.
> • Narrow (weak) AI does one kind of task (a spam filter, a chess program, a voice assistant); general AI that can do any intellectual task like a person does not yet exist.
> • AI systems need data, algorithms and computing power; they can make mistakes and copy biases in their data.

**Learn: Machines that seem to think** (draft)

> Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the ability of computer systems to do tasks that normally need human intelligence: recognising speech, understanding and translating language, recognising faces and objects, playing games and making decisions.
> A voice assistant on a phone, a translation app and a spam filter all use AI.

**Learn: Learning from data** (draft)

> Most modern AI uses machine learning. Instead of writing every rule, programmers give the system a lot of examples (data) and an algorithm that finds the patterns.
> A spam filter learns from thousands of e-mails marked 'spam' or 'not spam'; then it can sort new e-mails.
> AI needs three things: data, algorithms and computing power.

**Learn: What AI can and cannot do** (draft)

> All AI in use today is narrow: each system does one kind of task. A chess program cannot drive a car. General AI, able to do any task like a person, does not exist yet.
> AI can make mistakes and can copy unfairness hidden in its data, so its results must be checked by people.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **artificial intelligence**: computer systems doing tasks that need human-like intelligence
- **machine learning**: learning patterns from data
- **data**: facts and examples used to train a system
- **narrow AI**: AI designed for one kind of task

- Voice typing on a phone
- Face unlock
- Video recommendations

Common mistake: “Every computer program is AI” → “Only systems that learn or do intelligent tasks are AI”. A calculator follows fixed rules; it does not learn.

## Lesson 3: Societal Impact and Challenges of Applied AI

Prerequisites: 2 · Skill: s3-3 (Societal impact and challenges of applied AI)

**Note** (draft)

> Benefits of AI: faster diagnosis, better crop yields, help for disabled people (speech-to-text), saving time on routine work, new jobs (data scientists, AI trainers).
> Challenges:
> • Jobs: some routine jobs may be replaced; workers need new skills.
> • Privacy: AI uses large amounts of personal data, which can be misused.
> • Bias and fairness: AI trained on biased data can treat groups unfairly.
> • Misinformation: AI can create fake images, voices and videos (deepfakes) and convincing false text.
> • Over-dependence and errors: people may trust wrong answers; AI uses a lot of energy.
> Responsible use: protect personal data, check AI outputs, keep humans responsible for important decisions, follow laws and school rules.

**Learn: The good side** (draft)

> AI can save time and lives: it helps doctors detect disease early, farmers protect crops, banks stop fraud, and people with disabilities use phones and computers (speech-to-text, text-to-speech, image description).
> It also creates new jobs, such as data scientists and people who train and check AI systems.

**Learn: The challenges** (draft)

> • Jobs: some routine jobs may disappear or change; workers need to learn new skills.
> • Privacy: AI needs lots of data, often personal; it can be leaked, sold or misused.
> • Bias: AI trained on unbalanced data may treat some groups unfairly.
> • Fake content: deepfake videos, cloned voices and false articles can spread quickly.
> • Over-trust: AI can be confidently wrong; and big AI systems use a lot of electricity.

**Learn: Using AI responsibly** (draft)

> • Protect personal information: yours and other people's.
> • Check AI answers against reliable sources.
> • Do not present AI work as your own.
> • Verify shocking videos before sharing them.
> • Keep humans responsible for important decisions about people.

## Lesson 4: The use of appropriate Prompts to generate AI responses

Prerequisites: 1 · Skill: s3-4 (Writing appropriate prompts for AI tools)

**Note** (draft)

> A prompt is the instruction or question you give to an AI tool. Better prompts give better answers.
> A good prompt is:
> • clear and specific about the task;
> • gives context (who you are, what level, what for);
> • says the format you want (a list, a table, 100 words, steps);
> • may give an example or a role ('Explain as a Form 3 teacher…').
> Improve by iterating: read the answer, then refine the prompt.
> Never put passwords or private information in a prompt, and always check the answer.

**Learn: What a prompt is** (draft)

> When you use a chatbot or other AI tool, the instruction you type is the prompt. The AI's answer can only be as good as the prompt allows.
> 'Erosion' alone could mean many things. 'List three causes of soil erosion on farms in the North West region, with one way to prevent each' gets a useful answer.

**Learn: Parts of a good prompt** (draft)

> • Task: a clear verb, such as explain, list, compare or summarise.
> • Context: who you are and why ('I am a Form 3 student revising').
> • Format and length: bullet points, a table, 100 words, steps.
> • Role or example: 'Answer as a patient teacher', or show the style you want.
> If the answer is not right, iterate: change the prompt and try again.

**Learn: Safe and honest prompting** (draft)

> • Never type passwords, PINs, ID numbers or other people's private details.
> • Check answers: AI can invent facts.
> • Use AI to learn and practise, not to do your work for you. Handing in AI-written work as your own is dishonest.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **prompt**: the instruction or question given to an AI tool
- **context**: background information that guides the answer
- **iterate**: improve step by step
- **plagiarism**: presenting someone else's (or an AI's) work as your own

- 'Summarise in 50 words'
- 'Explain for Form 3'
- 'Give the answer as a table'

Common mistake: “Typing one word and expecting a perfect answer” → “Write a clear task with context and format”. The AI cannot guess what you need; tell it.

## Lesson 5: Robots and their applications

Prerequisites: 1 · Skill: s3-5 (Robots and their applications)

**Note** (draft)

> A robot is a programmable machine that can sense its surroundings and carry out actions automatically.
> Main parts: sensors (detect light, distance, touch, sound…), a controller/processor running a program, actuators (motors, grippers, wheels) and a power supply.
> Applications: manufacturing (welding, painting cars), medicine (surgical robots), homes (robot vacuum cleaners), agriculture (harvesting, spraying drones), exploration (Mars rovers, deep-sea robots), dangerous work (bomb disposal), warehouses and deliveries.
> Advantages: work non-stop, accurate, do dangerous or boring work. Disadvantages: expensive, need maintenance and power, may replace some jobs, cannot handle every unexpected situation.

**Learn: What makes a robot** (draft)

> A robot is a machine that can be programmed to sense what is around it and act on its own.
> • Sensors detect the surroundings: light, distance, touch, sound, temperature, cameras.
> • A controller (processor) runs a program that decides what to do.
> • Actuators carry out actions: motors, wheels, arms, grippers.
> • A power supply (battery or mains) runs it all.
> Sense → decide → act, again and again.

**Learn: Where robots work** (draft)

> • Industry: welding, painting and assembling cars, packing goods.
> • Medicine: surgical robots, robots that carry medicines in hospitals.
> • Homes: robot vacuum cleaners and lawn mowers.
> • Agriculture: drones that spray crops, harvesting robots.
> • Exploration and danger: Mars rovers, deep-sea robots, bomb-disposal robots, robots inside damaged nuclear plants.

**Learn: For and against** (draft)

> Advantages: they work without rest, very accurately, and can do dangerous, dirty or boring jobs.
> Disadvantages: they are expensive to buy, run and repair; they need power; they may replace some workers; and they can fail in situations their program did not expect.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **robot**: a programmable machine that senses and acts automatically
- **sensor**: a device that detects something in the surroundings
- **actuator**: a part that produces movement
- **controller**: the processor running the robot's program

- Car-factory welding robot

## Lesson 6: Applications of control systems, virtual reality and augmented reality

Prerequisites: 5 · Skill: s3-6 (Control systems, virtual reality and augmented reality)

**Note** (draft)

> A computer control system uses sensors to monitor conditions, a processor to compare readings with a set value, and actuators to make changes; the cycle repeats (feedback).
> Examples: automatic doors, traffic lights, air conditioning and thermostats, greenhouse watering, automatic street lights.
> Virtual reality (VR): a fully computer-generated world that the user enters with a headset; used for games, training pilots and surgeons, virtual tours.

## Lesson 8: The processor

Prerequisites: none · Skill: s3-8 (The processor (CPU): parts and performance)

**Note** (draft)

> The central processing unit (CPU, processor) carries out the instructions of programs. Main parts:
> • Control unit (CU): fetches instructions, decodes them and directs the other parts.
> • Arithmetic and logic unit (ALU): does calculations and comparisons.
> • Registers: tiny, very fast memory locations inside the CPU (e.g. program counter, accumulator).
> • Cache: small fast memory holding data the CPU uses often.
> Performance depends on: clock speed (in GHz: 1 GHz = 1 000 000 000 cycles per second), the number of cores (each core can process instructions at the same time), and cache size.

**Learn: The brain of the computer** (draft)

> The central processing unit (CPU), or processor, carries out the instructions of every program. It is a chip on the motherboard, usually under a fan and heat sink because it gets hot.
> Its main parts:
> • Control unit (CU): fetches each instruction from memory, decodes it, and directs the rest.
> • Arithmetic and logic unit (ALU): does calculations and comparisons.
> • Registers: tiny stores inside the CPU for the data being worked on now.
> • Cache: small, fast memory close to the CPU for data used often.

**Learn: Clock speed** (draft)

> The CPU works in steps timed by a clock. Clock speed is measured in hertz (cycles per second): 1 GHz (gigahertz) = 1000 MHz = 1 000 000 000 cycles per second.
> A 3 GHz processor has three billion clock cycles each second. A higher clock speed usually means faster processing.

**Learn: Cores and cache** (draft)

> Modern processors have several cores, each able to process instructions at the same time: dual-core (2), quad-core (4), octa-core (8).
> More cores help when running many programs at once. A bigger cache means the CPU waits less for data from RAM.
> So performance depends on clock speed, number of cores and cache size together.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **CPU**: the central processing unit, which carries out instructions
- **ALU**: arithmetic and logic unit
- **control unit**: part that fetches, decodes and directs
- **clock speed**: number of cycles per second, in GHz
- **core**: a processing unit inside the CPU

- 2.4 GHz = 2400 MHz
- Quad-core: 4 cores
- ALU compares two numbers

Common mistake: “The CPU stores all your files” → “Files are kept in secondary storage; the CPU processes instructions”. Registers and cache are tiny; long-term storage is on disks.

## Lesson 9: The processor cycle

Prerequisites: 8 · Skill: s3-9 (The fetch-decode-execute cycle)

**Note** (draft)

> The CPU repeats the fetch-decode-execute cycle billions of times a second:
> • Fetch: the control unit gets the next instruction from main memory (RAM), using the address in the program counter (PC); the PC then increases to point to the next instruction.
> • Decode: the control unit works out what the instruction means.
> • Execute: the instruction is carried out, e.g. the ALU adds two numbers and the result goes into the accumulator, or data is stored in memory.
> Then the cycle starts again with the next instruction. Programs and data are both kept in memory (the stored-program idea).

**Learn: One instruction at a time** (draft)

> A program is a list of instructions stored in main memory (RAM). The CPU carries them out one after another in a repeating cycle: fetch, decode, execute.
> At 3 GHz, a core can repeat this cycle billions of times every second.

**Learn: The three stages** (draft)

> • Fetch: the control unit looks at the program counter (PC), which holds the address of the next instruction, and copies that instruction from RAM into the CPU. The PC then increases by one.
> • Decode: the control unit works out what the instruction means and what is needed.
> • Execute: the instruction is carried out: the ALU may add or compare numbers and put the result in the accumulator, or data may be moved to or from memory.

**Learn: Why it matters** (draft)

> Everything a computer does, from showing a letter on the screen to playing a video, is built from millions of these simple cycles.
> Because programs and data are both stored in memory, the computer can switch from one program to another just by loading different instructions: this is the stored-program idea.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **fetch**: copy the next instruction from memory into the CPU
- **decode**: work out what the instruction means
- **execute**: carry out the instruction
- **program counter**: register holding the address of the next instruction
- **accumulator**: register that holds results from the ALU

- ADD: executed in the ALU
- PC: 100 → 101 after a fetch
- Cycle repeated billions of times a second

Common mistake: “The CPU decodes before fetching” → “The order is fetch, decode, execute”. The CPU must first get the instruction before it can understand it.

## Lesson 10: Primary storage

Prerequisites: 8 · Skill: s3-10 (Primary storage: RAM and ROM; storage units)

**Note** (draft)

> Primary (main) storage is directly accessed by the CPU.
> • RAM (random access memory): holds the programs and data in use; it can be read and written; it is volatile (contents lost when power goes off). More RAM lets more programs run smoothly.
> • ROM (read-only memory): holds the start-up instructions (BIOS/firmware); it can only be read; it is non-volatile.
> Units: 8 bits = 1 byte; 1 KB = 1024 bytes; 1 MB = 1024 KB; 1 GB = 1024 MB; 1 TB = 1024 GB.

**Learn: Memory the CPU uses directly** (draft)

## Lesson 13: Computer ports

Prerequisites: 12 · Skill: s3-13 (Computer ports and connectors)

**Note** (draft)

> A port is a socket where a device is connected to the computer.
> • USB (Universal Serial Bus): keyboards, mice, flash drives, printers, phones; USB-C is small and reversible and can also charge.
> • HDMI: high-definition video and sound to monitors, TVs and projectors. VGA: older analogue video port.
> • Ethernet (RJ45): wired network/internet cable.
> • Audio jacks (3.5 mm): headphones and microphones.
> • Power port: connects the charger or power cable.
> Data transfer speed is measured in bits or bytes per second; transfer time = file size ÷ transfer speed.

**Learn: Ports connect devices** (draft)

> Look at the side of a laptop or the back of a desktop: the sockets are ports. Each type of port is shaped for a certain kind of plug and job, so devices cannot easily be connected wrongly.

**Learn: Common ports** (draft)

> • USB: keyboards, mice, flash drives, printers, phones. USB-C is small, reversible and can carry data, video and power.
> • HDMI: digital video and sound to a monitor, TV or projector. VGA is an older video port still found on some projectors.
> • Ethernet (RJ45): a network cable to a router or switch.
> • Audio jacks (3.5 mm): headphones and microphones.
> • Power or charging port.

**Learn: How fast?** (draft)

> Ports and cables move data at different speeds, measured in megabytes per second (MB/s) or megabits per second.
> Transfer time = file size ÷ transfer speed. A 600 MB video at 40 MB/s takes 15 s; the same file at 10 MB/s takes 60 s.
> Change units first: 1 GB = 1024 MB.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **port**: a socket for connecting a device
- **USB**: Universal Serial Bus, for many devices
- **HDMI**: a port for video and sound
- **Ethernet**: a port for a wired network cable
- **transfer speed**: how much data moves each second

- Flash drive: USB
- Projector: HDMI
- Router cable: Ethernet
- 200 MB at 50 MB/s: 4 s

Common mistake: “Time = file size × speed” → “Time = file size ÷ speed”. A faster port must give a shorter time, so divide by the speed.

**Card c3s13-1** (draft)
