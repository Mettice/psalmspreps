**Learn: Where sulphur is found** (draft)

> Sulphur is found free (uncombined) in beds a few hundred metres underground, often near volcanoes and oil fields, notably in the United States, Mexico and Poland.

> • as sulphides: galena PbS, zinc blende ZnS, iron pyrites FeS₂;

**Learn: The Frasch process** (draft)

> Free sulphur deep underground is brought up by the Frasch pump, which has three concentric pipes.
> • Superheated water at about 170 °C is forced down the OUTER pipe. Pressure keeps it liquid above its normal boiling point, and it melts the sulphur, whose melting point is about 115 °C.
> • Hot compressed air is forced down the INNER pipe. It froths the molten sulphur.
> • The froth is less dense than water, so the pressure pushes it up the MIDDLE pipe to the surface, where it runs into tanks and solidifies.
> Because only the sulphur melts, the rock and sand stay underground and the sulphur comes up about 99 per cent pure.

- **froth**: a light mixture of gas bubbles and liquid

- gypsum CaSO₄: a sulphate

## Lesson 2: Properties and allotropes of Sulphur

Prerequisites: 1 · Skill: k4-2 (Describe the physical and chemical properties of sulphur and its allotropes)

**Note** (draft)

> Sulphur is a brittle yellow solid, insoluble in water, soluble in carbon disulphide, and a poor conductor of heat and electricity.
> Allotropes are different forms of the same element in the same physical state. Sulphur has two crystalline allotropes:
> • Rhombic (alpha) sulphur: octahedral crystals, stable below about 96 °C, density 2.07 g/cm³.
> • Monoclinic (beta) sulphur: needle-shaped crystals, stable between about 96 °C and the melting point, density 1.98 g/cm³.
> 96 °C is the transition temperature. Plastic sulphur, a brown rubbery form made by pouring boiling sulphur into cold water, is not crystalline and slowly changes back to rhombic sulphur.
> Chemically, sulphur burns in air with a blue flame to sulphur dioxide, and combines with many metals on heating to form sulphides.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to describe the properties of sulphur and tell its allotropes apart by crystal shape, stability and density.
> Builds on: Lesson 1 (where sulphur comes from) and Form Three structure and bonding.
> Leads to: Lessons 3 to 8, which deal with the compounds sulphur forms.

**Learn: Physical properties and allotropes** (draft)

> Sulphur is a brittle yellow solid. It is insoluble in water, dissolves in carbon disulphide, does not conduct electricity, and melts at about 115 °C.
> Allotropes are different forms of the same element in the same physical state. Sulphur has two crystalline forms:
> • Rhombic (alpha): octahedral crystals, density 2.07 g/cm³, stable below about 96 °C. Grown by letting carbon disulphide evaporate from a solution of sulphur.
> • Monoclinic (beta): needle-shaped crystals, density 1.98 g/cm³, stable between about 96 °C and the melting point. Grown by letting molten sulphur cool slowly and pouring off the liquid left.
> About 96 °C is the transition temperature, where one form changes into the other.

**Learn: Plastic sulphur and chemical properties** (draft)

> If boiling sulphur is poured into cold water it sets as plastic sulphur: a brown rubbery solid with no crystals. It is unstable and slowly turns back into rhombic sulphur.
> Chemically, sulphur:
> • burns in air with a blue flame to sulphur dioxide: S + O₂ → SO₂;
> • combines with many metals on heating to give sulphides: Fe + S → FeS;
> • is oxidised by hot concentrated sulphuric acid and by hot concentrated nitric acid.
> It does not react with water or with dilute acids.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **allotropes**: different forms of the same element in the same physical state
- **rhombic sulphur**: the octahedral form, stable below about 96 °C
- **monoclinic sulphur**: the needle-shaped form, stable above about 96 °C
- **transition temperature**: the temperature at which one allotrope changes into the other

- rhombic: octahedral, 2.07 g/cm³
- monoclinic: needles, 1.98 g/cm³
- S + O₂ → SO₂

Common mistake: “rhombic and monoclinic sulphur are isotopes of sulphur” → “they are allotropes”. Isotopes differ in the nucleus. Allotropes differ in how the same atoms are arranged.

## Lesson 4: Chemical properties of SO2

Prerequisites: 3 · Skill: k4-4 (Describe the chemical properties of sulphur dioxide)

**Note** (draft)

> Sulphur dioxide is an acidic oxide, a reducing agent and a bleaching agent.
> • Acidic oxide: SO2 + H2O → H2SO3 (sulphurous acid), which turns blue litmus red. With alkalis it gives sulphites: SO2 + 2NaOH → Na2SO3 + H2O.
> • Reducing agent: it decolourises acidified potassium manganate(VII) (purple to colourless) and turns acidified potassium dichromate(VI) from orange to green. It also decolourises bromine water.
> • Bleaching agent: it bleaches by reduction, so the bleaching is temporary; the colour slowly returns as air oxidises the material. Chlorine bleaches by oxidation and is permanent.
> • As an oxidising agent with a stronger reducing agent: SO2 + 2H2S → 3S + 2H2O, giving a yellow deposit of sulphur.
> In the air, SO2 is oxidised to SO3 and dissolves in rain as sulphuric acid: this is acid rain.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to describe sulphur dioxide as an acidic oxide, a reducing agent, a bleach and sometimes an oxidising agent.
> Builds on: Lesson 3 (preparing SO₂) and Form Three acids, bases and redox.
> Leads to: Lesson 8 (sulphuric acid) and the problem of acid rain.

**Learn: An acidic oxide and a reducing agent** (draft)

> Sulphur dioxide is the oxide of a non-metal, so it is acidic.
> • With water: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃, sulphurous acid, which turns blue litmus red.
> • With an alkali: SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O, giving a sulphite.
> It is also a good reducing agent, and the colour changes are used as tests:
> • acidified potassium manganate(VII): purple → colourless;
> • acidified potassium dichromate(VI): orange → green;
> • bromine water: brown → colourless.
> In each case the SO₂ is itself oxidised to a sulphate.

**Learn: Bleaching, oxidising and acid rain** (draft)

> Sulphur dioxide bleaches moist coloured materials such as flowers, straw, wool and paper. It does so by REDUCTION, so oxygen in the air slowly turns the colour back: the bleaching is temporary. Chlorine bleaches by oxidation, and that bleaching is permanent.
> With a stronger reducing agent, SO₂ is itself the oxidising agent:
> SO₂ + 2H₂S → 3S + 2H₂O, seen as a pale yellow deposit of sulphur.
> In the air, sulphur dioxide from burning coal and oil is oxidised and dissolves in rain as sulphuric acid. This acid rain damages buildings, kills fish in lakes and harms crops.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **acidic oxide**: an oxide that gives an acid with water and a salt with an alkali
- **reducing agent**: a substance that removes oxygen from, or gives electrons to, another
- **temporary bleach**: a bleach whose effect is slowly reversed by air
- **acid rain**: rain made acidic by dissolved oxides of sulphur and nitrogen

## Lesson 6: Chemical properties of H2S

Prerequisites: 5 · Skill: k4-6 (Describe the chemical properties of hydrogen sulphide)

**Note** (draft)

> Hydrogen sulphide is a weak acid, a reducing agent and it burns in air.
> • Weak acid: its solution turns blue litmus red. With alkalis it gives sulphides: H2S + 2NaOH → Na2S + 2H2O.
> • Burning: in plenty of air, 2H2S + 3O2 → 2SO2 + 2H2O (blue flame). In a limited supply, 2H2S + O2 → 2S + 2H2O, leaving a yellow deposit.
> • Reducing agent: it is oxidised to sulphur by many oxidising agents. With acidified potassium manganate(VII) the purple colour goes and sulphur appears; with chlorine water the colour goes; with sulphur dioxide: SO2 + 2H2S → 3S + 2H2O.
> • With metal salts it gives coloured sulphide precipitates, used in analysis: lead(II) nitrate gives black PbS. Lead(II) ethanoate paper turning black is the test for the gas.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to describe hydrogen sulphide as a weak acid, a reducing agent and a gas that burns, and give the test for it.
> Builds on: Lesson 5 (preparing H₂S) and Lesson 4 (redox reactions of SO₂).
> Leads to: Lesson 7 (the sulphides themselves) and the use of H₂S in analysis.

**Learn: Burning and acidity** (draft)

> Hydrogen sulphide burns in air with a blue flame, and what it gives depends on how much oxygen it has.
> • Plenty of air: 2H₂S + 3O₂ → 2SO₂ + 2H₂O.
> • Limited air: 2H₂S + O₂ → 2S + 2H₂O, leaving a yellow deposit.
> In water it is a weak acid: the solution turns blue litmus red, and with sodium hydroxide it gives a sulphide:
> H₂S + 2NaOH → Na₂S + 2H₂O.

**Learn: A reducing agent, and the test** (draft)

> Hydrogen sulphide gives up hydrogen easily, so it is a good reducing agent. It is itself oxidised to sulphur, which appears as a pale yellow deposit:
> • acidified potassium manganate(VII): purple → colourless, with sulphur;
> • chlorine water: the colour goes, with sulphur;
> • sulphur dioxide: SO₂ + 2H₂S → 3S + 2H₂O. Here SO₂ is the oxidising agent.
> With solutions of metal salts it gives sulphides of different colours, which is why it is used in analysis:
> Pb(NO₃)₂ + H₂S → PbS + 2HNO₃, a BLACK precipitate.
> The test for the gas is damp lead(II) ethanoate paper, which turns black.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **reducing agent**: a substance that gives electrons or hydrogen to another
- **precipitate**: an insoluble solid formed when two solutions are mixed

## Lesson 8: Chemical properties of sulphuric acid

Prerequisites: 4 · Skill: k4-8 (Describe the chemical properties of sulphuric acid)

**Note** (draft)

> Dilute sulphuric acid behaves as a typical strong acid:
> • with a metal above hydrogen: Zn + H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2;
> • with a base or basic oxide: CuO + H2SO4 → CuSO4 + H2O;
> • with a carbonate: Na2CO3 + H2SO4 → Na2SO4 + H2O + CO2.
> Concentrated sulphuric acid has three further properties:
> • Dehydrating agent: it removes hydrogen and oxygen as water. Sugar turns to a black spongy mass of carbon; blue copper(II) sulphate crystals turn white.
> • Oxidising agent when hot: Cu + 2H2SO4 → CuSO4 + SO2 + 2H2O.
> • A less volatile acid: it displaces more volatile acids from their salts. NaCl + H2SO4 → NaHSO4 + HCl.
> Safety: always add the acid to water, never water to the acid, because a great deal of heat is given out.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to describe dilute sulphuric acid as a typical acid and concentrated sulphuric acid as a dehydrating, oxidising and less volatile acid.
> Builds on: Lesson 4 (SO₂ and the acid made from it) and Form Three acids, bases and salts.
> Leads to: Lesson 9 (the uses of sulphur and its compounds) and salt preparations.

**Learn: The dilute acid** (draft)

> Dilute sulphuric acid behaves as a typical strong acid. It turns blue litmus red and reacts in the usual three ways:
> • with a metal above hydrogen: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂;
> • with a base or a basic oxide: CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O;
> • with a carbonate: Na₂CO₃ + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O + CO₂.
> Its salts are sulphates. Lead and barium sulphates are insoluble, which is why barium chloride solution with dilute hydrochloric acid is the test for a sulphate.

**Learn: The concentrated acid** (draft)

> Concentrated sulphuric acid has three extra properties:
> • DEHYDRATING agent: it removes hydrogen and oxygen as water. Sugar becomes a black spongy mass of carbon; blue copper(II) sulphate crystals turn white; paper and wood char. It also dries gases that do not react with it.
> • OXIDISING agent when hot: Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O. Copper is below hydrogen, so it could not react with the dilute acid at all.
> • LESS VOLATILE acid: on heating it drives more volatile acids out of their salts: NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl.
> Safety: always add the acid slowly to water with stirring, never water to the acid, because a great deal of heat is given out.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **dehydrating agent**: a substance that removes hydrogen and oxygen as water
- **oxidising agent**: a substance that gives oxygen to, or takes electrons from, another
- **volatile**: easily turned into a vapour
- **water of crystallisation**: water joined into a crystal, as in blue CuSO₄ crystals

- Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
- sugar → black carbon
- Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O

## Lesson 11: Occurrence and properties of nitrogen

Prerequisites: none · Skill: k4-11 (Describe the occurrence and properties of nitrogen)

**Note** (draft)

> Nitrogen makes up about 78 per cent of the air by volume. It is also combined in proteins, in nitrates in the soil, and in ammonia.
> Laboratory preparation from air: remove carbon dioxide with sodium hydroxide, dry the air, then pass it over heated copper, which takes the oxygen. What is left is nitrogen with a little argon, so it is slightly denser than pure nitrogen.
> Pure nitrogen is made by heating ammonium nitrite: NH4NO2 → N2 + 2H2O.
> In industry it comes from the fractional distillation of liquid air.
> Properties: a colourless, odourless gas, almost insoluble in water, slightly less dense than air, and very unreactive because of the strong triple bond N≡N. It does not burn and does not support burning. It combines with magnesium (3Mg + N2 → Mg3N2), with hydrogen in the Haber process, and with oxygen in a lightning flash.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

**Learn: Where nitrogen is found, and how it is obtained** (draft)

> Nitrogen makes up about 78 per cent of the air by volume. It is also combined in the proteins of living things, in nitrates in the soil, and in ammonia.
> From air in the laboratory: pass air through sodium hydroxide solution to take out carbon dioxide, then through concentrated sulphuric acid to dry it, then over heated copper, which takes the oxygen:
> 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO
> What is left is nitrogen with a little argon, so it is slightly DENSER than pure nitrogen.
> Pure nitrogen: heat ammonium nitrite, NH₄NO₂ → N₂ + 2H₂O.
> In industry: fractional distillation of liquid air, where nitrogen boils off first.

**Learn: Properties** (draft)

> Nitrogen is a colourless, odourless gas, almost insoluble in water, and very slightly less dense than air (relative molecular mass 28 against about 29 for air).
> It is very unreactive. Its two atoms are joined by a TRIPLE bond, N≡N, which needs a great deal of energy to break. It does not burn and does not support burning.
> It does react in hard conditions:
> • with burning magnesium: 3Mg + N₂ → Mg₃N₂;
> • with hydrogen under pressure and a catalyst (the Haber process): N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃;
> • with oxygen in a lightning flash or a hot engine: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO.
> Being unreactive makes it useful: packets of food and oil tanks are filled with it to keep oxygen out.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **triple bond**: three shared pairs of electrons between two atoms
- **inert**: very unreactive
- **fractional distillation**: separating liquids by their different boiling points
- **nitride**: a compound of a metal with nitrogen, such as Mg₃N₂

- air: about 78 per cent nitrogen
- 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO
- 3Mg + N₂ → Mg₃N₂

Common mistake: “nitrogen is unreactive because its atoms have full outer shells” → “it is unreactive because of the strong triple bond”. A nitrogen ATOM has 5 outer electrons. It is the N≡N bond in the molecule that is hard to break.

## Lesson 13: Lab. preparation and properties of nitric acid

**Note** (draft)

> Laboratory preparation: heat potassium or sodium nitrate with concentrated sulphuric acid in an all-glass apparatus and distil off the nitric acid.
> KNO3 + H2SO4 → KHSO4 + HNO3
> The less volatile sulphuric acid drives out the more volatile nitric acid. Glass is used because the hot acid attacks cork, rubber and metals.
> Industrially it is made by oxidising ammonia (the Ostwald process).

> • As an acid: with bases, basic oxides and carbonates it gives nitrates. Na2CO3 + 2HNO3 → 2NaNO3 + H2O + CO2.
> • As an oxidising agent: with metals it gives a nitrate and an oxide of nitrogen, not hydrogen. Dilute acid with copper gives NO; concentrated acid gives NO2.
> • It decomposes in light: 4HNO3 → 4NO2 + 2H2O + O2, so pure acid slowly turns yellow and is kept in dark bottles.

## Lesson 14: Effect of heat on nitrates

Prerequisites: 13 · Skill: k4-14 (Describe the effect of heat on nitrates)

**Note** (draft)

> All nitrates decompose on heating, but what is left depends on the metal.
> • Sodium and potassium nitrates (very reactive metals) give the nitrite and oxygen:
> 2NaNO3 → 2NaNO2 + O2.
> • Most other metal nitrates give the metal oxide, brown nitrogen dioxide and oxygen:
> 2Pb(NO3)2 → 2PbO + 4NO2 + O2. Lead(II) nitrate crackles as it decomposes.
> • Silver and mercury nitrates (very unreactive metals) give the metal itself, nitrogen dioxide and oxygen:
> 2AgNO3 → 2Ag + 2NO2 + O2.
> • Ammonium nitrate is not a metal nitrate: it gives dinitrogen oxide and water, NH4NO3 → N2O + 2H2O.
> The more reactive the metal, the more stable its nitrate is to heat.

**Learn: Three patterns** (draft)

> Every nitrate decomposes on heating, but the products depend on how reactive the metal is.
> • VERY REACTIVE metals (sodium, potassium): the nitrite and oxygen only.

## Lesson 15: Oxides of Nitrogen: (Nitrogen dioxide, Nitrogen oxide and Dinitrogen oxide) Preparation and properties

Prerequisites: 14 · Skill: k4-15 (Prepare and describe the oxides of nitrogen)

**Note** (draft)

> Three oxides of nitrogen are studied.
> • Dinitrogen oxide, N2O ('laughing gas'): made by gently heating ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3 → N2O + 2H2O. Colourless, faintly sweet, fairly soluble, neutral. It relights a glowing splint, like oxygen, because it decomposes on the hot splint. Used with oxygen as a mild anaesthetic.
> • Nitrogen oxide, NO: made by warming copper with dilute nitric acid. Colourless and neutral, almost insoluble in water, but it turns brown at once in air: 2NO + O2 → 2NO2. That is the test for it.
> • Nitrogen dioxide, NO2: made by heating lead(II) nitrate, or by copper with concentrated nitric acid. A brown, poisonous, acidic gas, denser than air, very soluble: 2NO2 + H2O → HNO3 + HNO2.
> Nitrogen oxides from car engines and lightning take part in acid rain.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

**Learn: Dinitrogen oxide and nitrogen oxide** (draft)

## Lesson 16: Lesson Lesson 15. The nitrogen cycle

Prerequisites: 15 · Skill: k4-16 (Describe the nitrogen cycle)

**Note** (draft)

> The nitrogen cycle is how nitrogen passes between the air, the soil and living things.
> • Fixation: nitrogen from the air is turned into compounds plants can use. By nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) in the root nodules of legumes such as beans and groundnuts; by lightning, which makes nitrogen oxides that reach the soil in rain; and by industry, in the Haber process.
> • Absorption: plant roots take in nitrates and build them into proteins. Animals get their protein by eating plants.
> • Decay: bacteria of decay break down dead plants, animals and waste into ammonium compounds.
> • Nitrification: nitrifying bacteria change ammonium compounds into nitrites (Nitrosomonas) and then nitrates (Nitrobacter).
> • Denitrification: in waterlogged soil, denitrifying bacteria change nitrates back into nitrogen gas, which returns to the air.
> Farmers keep the soil rich by rotating crops with legumes and by adding fertilisers.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to name and explain each stage of the nitrogen cycle and say how farming practices affect it.
> Builds on: Lesson 11 (nitrogen is unreactive) and Form Three food chains.
> Leads to: Lesson 17 (fertilisers) and the study of soil fertility in Biology and Agriculture.

**Learn: Getting nitrogen into living things** (draft)

> Nitrogen gas is 78 per cent of the air, but it is so unreactive that plants cannot use it. It must first be FIXED into a compound:
> • nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) in the root nodules of legumes such as beans, groundnuts and soya;
> • lightning: the flash is hot enough to make N₂ and O₂ combine, and the oxides dissolve in rain;
> • industry: the Haber process makes ammonia, which is turned into fertiliser.
> Plant roots absorb NITRATES from the soil and build them into proteins. Animals get their protein by eating plants.

**Learn: Returning nitrogen to the soil and the air** (draft)

> • DECAY: when plants and animals die, and in their waste, bacteria of decay break the protein down into ammonium compounds.
> • NITRIFICATION: nitrifying bacteria change these into nitrites (Nitrosomonas) and then into nitrates (Nitrobacter), which plants can use again.
> • DENITRIFICATION: in waterlogged soil, where there is little air, denitrifying bacteria change nitrates back into nitrogen gas, and the soil loses nitrogen to the air.
> Farmers work with the cycle: they rotate crops with legumes, plough crop residues back in, drain waterlogged land, and add fertilisers to replace the nitrogen taken away in the harvest.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **nitrogen fixation**: changing nitrogen gas into a usable compound
- **legume**: a plant such as a bean or groundnut with nitrogen-fixing root nodules
- **nitrification**: the change of ammonium compounds into nitrates by bacteria
- **denitrification**: the change of nitrates back into nitrogen gas

- Rhizobium in bean roots: fixation
- decay → ammonium compounds
- waterlogged soil: denitrification

Common mistake: “plants take nitrogen straight from the air” → “they take nitrates from the soil”. Only certain bacteria can use nitrogen gas; plants need it already fixed.

## Lesson 17: Uses of nitrogen compounds

Prerequisites: 16 · Skill: k4-17 (State the uses of nitrogen and its compounds)

**Note** (draft)

> Nitrogen gas: filling packets of food and flushing oil tanks, because it is unreactive; as liquid nitrogen at −196 °C for quick-freezing food and storing medical samples.
> Ammonia: made in the Haber process and used to make fertilisers (ammonium sulphate, ammonium nitrate, urea), nitric acid (the Ostwald process), cleaning liquids, and as a refrigerant.
> Nitric acid: making fertilisers, explosives (TNT, nitroglycerine), dyes, drugs and plastics.
> Fertilisers replace the nitrogen taken out of the soil by crops, so yields stay high. Too much, however, is washed into rivers, where it makes water plants grow too fast; when these die and decay the water loses its oxygen and fish die. This is eutrophication.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to give the uses of nitrogen, ammonia and nitric acid, and explain how too much fertiliser harms rivers.
> Builds on: Lessons 11 to 16, which give the properties behind each use.
> Leads to: the mole calculations of the next chapter, used to work out how much fertiliser a crop needs.

**Learn: Uses** (draft)

> NITROGEN GAS is used where an unreactive atmosphere is needed: filling packets of crisps and other food so the fat does not go rancid, and flushing out oil tanks so that they cannot catch fire. As LIQUID nitrogen at −196 °C it quick-freezes food and stores blood and other medical samples.
> AMMONIA, made in the Haber process, is the starting point for:
> • fertilisers: ammonium sulphate, ammonium nitrate and urea;
> • nitric acid, by the Ostwald process;
> • cleaning liquids, and refrigeration, because it takes in much heat as it evaporates.
> NITRIC ACID is used for fertilisers, explosives such as TNT, dyes, drugs and plastics.
> DINITROGEN OXIDE is used with oxygen as a mild anaesthetic.

**Learn: Fertilisers and the environment** (draft)

> Every harvest takes nitrogen off the land in the grain or the leaves. Fertilisers put it back, so yields stay high and the same field can be used year after year.
> A fertiliser is judged partly by its percentage of nitrogen by mass: in NH₄NO₃ the nitrogen is 28 out of 80, which is 35 per cent.
> But nitrates are soluble, so rain washes them into streams. There they make water plants grow very fast; when these die, the bacteria that decay them use up the oxygen and the fish suffocate. This is eutrophication.
> Careful farmers use only what the crop needs and leave strips of grass beside streams.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **fertiliser**: a substance added to soil to supply the elements plants need
- **Haber process**: making ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen
- **eutrophication**: the loss of oxygen in water caused by too many nutrients
- **urea**: CO(NH₂)₂, a nitrogen fertiliser made from ammonia and carbon dioxide
