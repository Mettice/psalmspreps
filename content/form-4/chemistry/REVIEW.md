**Learn: Where sulphur is found** (draft)

> Sulphur is found free (uncombined) in beds a few hundred metres underground, often near volcanoes and oil fields, notably in the United States, Mexico and Poland.

> • as sulphides: galena PbS, zinc blende ZnS, iron pyrites FeS₂;

> Sulphur is also present in coal and crude oil, and in the proteins of living things.

**Learn: The Frasch process** (draft)

> Free sulphur deep underground is brought up by the Frasch pump, which has three concentric pipes.
> • Superheated water at about 170 °C is forced down the OUTER pipe. Pressure keeps it liquid above its normal boiling point, and it melts the sulphur, whose melting point is about 115 °C.
> • Hot compressed air is forced down the INNER pipe. It froths the molten sulphur.
> • The froth is less dense than water, so the pressure pushes it up the MIDDLE pipe to the surface, where it runs into tanks and solidifies.
> Because only the sulphur melts, the rock and sand stay underground and the sulphur comes up about 99 per cent pure.

- **froth**: a light mixture of gas bubbles and liquid

- gypsum CaSO₄: a sulphate

## Lesson 2: Properties and allotropes of Sulphur

Prerequisites: 1 · Skill: k4-2 (Describe the physical and chemical properties of sulphur and its allotropes)

**Note** (draft)

> Sulphur is a brittle yellow solid, insoluble in water, soluble in carbon disulphide, and a poor conductor of heat and electricity.
> Allotropes are different forms of the same element in the same physical state. Sulphur has two crystalline allotropes:
> • Rhombic (alpha) sulphur: octahedral crystals, stable below about 96 °C, density 2.07 g/cm³.
> • Monoclinic (beta) sulphur: needle-shaped crystals, stable between about 96 °C and the melting point, density 1.98 g/cm³.
> 96 °C is the transition temperature. Plastic sulphur, a brown rubbery form made by pouring boiling sulphur into cold water, is not crystalline and slowly changes back to rhombic sulphur.
> Chemically, sulphur burns in air with a blue flame to sulphur dioxide, and combines with many metals on heating to form sulphides.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to describe the properties of sulphur and tell its allotropes apart by crystal shape, stability and density.
> Builds on: Lesson 1 (where sulphur comes from) and Form Three structure and bonding.
> Leads to: Lessons 3 to 8, which deal with the compounds sulphur forms.

**Learn: Physical properties and allotropes** (draft)

> Sulphur is a brittle yellow solid. It is insoluble in water, dissolves in carbon disulphide, does not conduct electricity, and melts at about 115 °C.
> Allotropes are different forms of the same element in the same physical state. Sulphur has two crystalline forms:
> • Rhombic (alpha): octahedral crystals, density 2.07 g/cm³, stable below about 96 °C. Grown by letting carbon disulphide evaporate from a solution of sulphur.
> • Monoclinic (beta): needle-shaped crystals, density 1.98 g/cm³, stable between about 96 °C and the melting point. Grown by letting molten sulphur cool slowly and pouring off the liquid left.
> About 96 °C is the transition temperature, where one form changes into the other.

**Learn: Plastic sulphur and chemical properties** (draft)

> If boiling sulphur is poured into cold water it sets as plastic sulphur: a brown rubbery solid with no crystals. It is unstable and slowly turns back into rhombic sulphur.
> Chemically, sulphur:
> • burns in air with a blue flame to sulphur dioxide: S + O₂ → SO₂;
> • combines with many metals on heating to give sulphides: Fe + S → FeS;
> • is oxidised by hot concentrated sulphuric acid and by hot concentrated nitric acid.
> It does not react with water or with dilute acids.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **allotropes**: different forms of the same element in the same physical state
- **rhombic sulphur**: the octahedral form, stable below about 96 °C
- **monoclinic sulphur**: the needle-shaped form, stable above about 96 °C
- **transition temperature**: the temperature at which one allotrope changes into the other

- rhombic: octahedral, 2.07 g/cm³
- monoclinic: needles, 1.98 g/cm³
- S + O₂ → SO₂

Common mistake: “rhombic and monoclinic sulphur are isotopes of sulphur” → “they are allotropes”. Isotopes differ in the nucleus. Allotropes differ in how the same atoms are arranged.

## Lesson 4: Chemical properties of SO2

Prerequisites: 3 · Skill: k4-4 (Describe the chemical properties of sulphur dioxide)

**Note** (draft)

> Sulphur dioxide is an acidic oxide, a reducing agent and a bleaching agent.
> • Acidic oxide: SO2 + H2O → H2SO3 (sulphurous acid), which turns blue litmus red. With alkalis it gives sulphites: SO2 + 2NaOH → Na2SO3 + H2O.
> • Reducing agent: it decolourises acidified potassium manganate(VII) (purple to colourless) and turns acidified potassium dichromate(VI) from orange to green. It also decolourises bromine water.
> • Bleaching agent: it bleaches by reduction, so the bleaching is temporary; the colour slowly returns as air oxidises the material. Chlorine bleaches by oxidation and is permanent.
> • As an oxidising agent with a stronger reducing agent: SO2 + 2H2S → 3S + 2H2O, giving a yellow deposit of sulphur.
> In the air, SO2 is oxidised to SO3 and dissolves in rain as sulphuric acid: this is acid rain.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to describe sulphur dioxide as an acidic oxide, a reducing agent, a bleach and sometimes an oxidising agent.
> Builds on: Lesson 3 (preparing SO₂) and Form Three acids, bases and redox.
> Leads to: Lesson 8 (sulphuric acid) and the problem of acid rain.

**Learn: An acidic oxide and a reducing agent** (draft)

> Sulphur dioxide is the oxide of a non-metal, so it is acidic.
> • With water: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃, sulphurous acid, which turns blue litmus red.
> • With an alkali: SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O, giving a sulphite.
> It is also a good reducing agent, and the colour changes are used as tests:
> • acidified potassium manganate(VII): purple → colourless;
> • acidified potassium dichromate(VI): orange → green;
> • bromine water: brown → colourless.
> In each case the SO₂ is itself oxidised to a sulphate.

**Learn: Bleaching, oxidising and acid rain** (draft)

> Sulphur dioxide bleaches moist coloured materials such as flowers, straw, wool and paper. It does so by REDUCTION, so oxygen in the air slowly turns the colour back: the bleaching is temporary. Chlorine bleaches by oxidation, and that bleaching is permanent.
> With a stronger reducing agent, SO₂ is itself the oxidising agent:
> SO₂ + 2H₂S → 3S + 2H₂O, seen as a pale yellow deposit of sulphur.
> In the air, sulphur dioxide from burning coal and oil is oxidised and dissolves in rain as sulphuric acid. This acid rain damages buildings, kills fish in lakes and harms crops.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **acidic oxide**: an oxide that gives an acid with water and a salt with an alkali
- **reducing agent**: a substance that removes oxygen from, or gives electrons to, another
- **temporary bleach**: a bleach whose effect is slowly reversed by air
- **acid rain**: rain made acidic by dissolved oxides of sulphur and nitrogen

## Lesson 6: Chemical properties of H2S

Prerequisites: 5 · Skill: k4-6 (Describe the chemical properties of hydrogen sulphide)

**Note** (draft)

> Hydrogen sulphide is a weak acid, a reducing agent and it burns in air.
> • Weak acid: its solution turns blue litmus red. With alkalis it gives sulphides: H2S + 2NaOH → Na2S + 2H2O.
> • Burning: in plenty of air, 2H2S + 3O2 → 2SO2 + 2H2O (blue flame). In a limited supply, 2H2S + O2 → 2S + 2H2O, leaving a yellow deposit.
> • Reducing agent: it is oxidised to sulphur by many oxidising agents. With acidified potassium manganate(VII) the purple colour goes and sulphur appears; with chlorine water the colour goes; with sulphur dioxide: SO2 + 2H2S → 3S + 2H2O.
> • With metal salts it gives coloured sulphide precipitates, used in analysis: lead(II) nitrate gives black PbS. Lead(II) ethanoate paper turning black is the test for the gas.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to describe hydrogen sulphide as a weak acid, a reducing agent and a gas that burns, and give the test for it.
> Builds on: Lesson 5 (preparing H₂S) and Lesson 4 (redox reactions of SO₂).
> Leads to: Lesson 7 (the sulphides themselves) and the use of H₂S in analysis.

**Learn: Burning and acidity** (draft)

> Hydrogen sulphide burns in air with a blue flame, and what it gives depends on how much oxygen it has.
> • Plenty of air: 2H₂S + 3O₂ → 2SO₂ + 2H₂O.
> • Limited air: 2H₂S + O₂ → 2S + 2H₂O, leaving a yellow deposit.
> In water it is a weak acid: the solution turns blue litmus red, and with sodium hydroxide it gives a sulphide:
> H₂S + 2NaOH → Na₂S + 2H₂O.

**Learn: A reducing agent, and the test** (draft)

> Hydrogen sulphide gives up hydrogen easily, so it is a good reducing agent. It is itself oxidised to sulphur, which appears as a pale yellow deposit:
> • acidified potassium manganate(VII): purple → colourless, with sulphur;
> • chlorine water: the colour goes, with sulphur;
> • sulphur dioxide: SO₂ + 2H₂S → 3S + 2H₂O. Here SO₂ is the oxidising agent.
> With solutions of metal salts it gives sulphides of different colours, which is why it is used in analysis:
> Pb(NO₃)₂ + H₂S → PbS + 2HNO₃, a BLACK precipitate.
> The test for the gas is damp lead(II) ethanoate paper, which turns black.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **weak acid**: an acid only slightly split into ions in water
- **reducing agent**: a substance that gives electrons or hydrogen to another
- **precipitate**: an insoluble solid formed when two solutions are mixed
- **lead(II) ethanoate paper**: test paper that turns black with hydrogen sulphide

- 2H₂S + 3O₂ → 2SO₂ + 2H₂O
- PbS: black
- sulphur: a pale yellow deposit

## Lesson 8: Chemical properties of sulphuric acid

Prerequisites: 4 · Skill: k4-8 (Describe the chemical properties of sulphuric acid)

**Note** (draft)

> Dilute sulphuric acid behaves as a typical strong acid:
> • with a metal above hydrogen: Zn + H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2;
> • with a base or basic oxide: CuO + H2SO4 → CuSO4 + H2O;
> • with a carbonate: Na2CO3 + H2SO4 → Na2SO4 + H2O + CO2.
> Concentrated sulphuric acid has three further properties:
> • Dehydrating agent: it removes hydrogen and oxygen as water. Sugar turns to a black spongy mass of carbon; blue copper(II) sulphate crystals turn white.
> • Oxidising agent when hot: Cu + 2H2SO4 → CuSO4 + SO2 + 2H2O.
> • A less volatile acid: it displaces more volatile acids from their salts. NaCl + H2SO4 → NaHSO4 + HCl.
> Safety: always add the acid to water, never water to the acid, because a great deal of heat is given out.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to describe dilute sulphuric acid as a typical acid and concentrated sulphuric acid as a dehydrating, oxidising and less volatile acid.
> Builds on: Lesson 4 (SO₂ and the acid made from it) and Form Three acids, bases and salts.
> Leads to: Lesson 9 (the uses of sulphur and its compounds) and salt preparations.

**Learn: The dilute acid** (draft)

> Dilute sulphuric acid behaves as a typical strong acid. It turns blue litmus red and reacts in the usual three ways:
> • with a metal above hydrogen: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂;
> • with a base or a basic oxide: CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O;
> • with a carbonate: Na₂CO₃ + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O + CO₂.
> Its salts are sulphates. Lead and barium sulphates are insoluble, which is why barium chloride solution with dilute hydrochloric acid is the test for a sulphate.

**Learn: The concentrated acid** (draft)

> Concentrated sulphuric acid has three extra properties:
> • DEHYDRATING agent: it removes hydrogen and oxygen as water. Sugar becomes a black spongy mass of carbon; blue copper(II) sulphate crystals turn white; paper and wood char. It also dries gases that do not react with it.
> • OXIDISING agent when hot: Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O. Copper is below hydrogen, so it could not react with the dilute acid at all.
> • LESS VOLATILE acid: on heating it drives more volatile acids out of their salts: NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl.
> Safety: always add the acid slowly to water with stirring, never water to the acid, because a great deal of heat is given out.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **dehydrating agent**: a substance that removes hydrogen and oxygen as water
- **oxidising agent**: a substance that gives oxygen to, or takes electrons from, another
- **volatile**: easily turned into a vapour
- **water of crystallisation**: water joined into a crystal, as in blue CuSO₄ crystals

- Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
- sugar → black carbon
- Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O

## Lesson 9: Uses of sulphur and its compounds

Prerequisites: 8 · Skill: k4-9 (State the uses of sulphur and its compounds)

**Note** (draft)

> Sulphur itself is used to:
> • make sulphuric acid, by far its largest use (the Contact process);
> • vulcanise rubber, linking the chains so that tyres are harder, stronger and more elastic;
> • make matches, gunpowder and fireworks, because it catches fire easily;
> • make fungicides, dusted or sprayed on crops;
> • make drugs and ointments for skin diseases.
> Sulphur dioxide is used to bleach wool, silk, straw and paper, to preserve dried fruit and fruit juice, and to make sulphuric acid.
> Sulphuric acid is used in fertilisers (ammonium sulphate), detergents, paints, dyes, plastics, car batteries, pickling steel before plating, and in refining petroleum. Because so many industries depend on it, the amount a country uses is a measure of how industrial it is.

**Learn: Sulphur and sulphur dioxide** (draft)

> Sulphur itself is used to:
> • make sulphuric acid, by far its largest use;
> • vulcanise rubber: heating rubber with sulphur links its chains, so tyres are harder, stronger and more elastic;
> • make matches, gunpowder and fireworks, because it catches fire easily;
> • make fungicides, as dust or as a lime-sulphur spray on crops;
> • make ointments and drugs for skin diseases.
> Sulphur dioxide bleaches wool, silk, straw and paper, which chlorine would damage. It also preserves dried fruit and fruit juice, because it kills bacteria and moulds.

**Learn: Sulphuric acid** (draft)

> Sulphuric acid is one of the most important chemicals made. It is used:
> • to make fertilisers, especially ammonium sulphate from ammonia;
> • to make detergents, paints, dyes, plastics and fibres;
> • as the electrolyte in lead-acid car batteries;
> • to pickle steel, cleaning the surface before it is plated;
> • in refining petroleum.
> Because so many industries depend on it, the amount a country uses is often taken as a measure of how industrial it is. Care is still needed: the acid is corrosive, and sulphur dioxide escaping from factories causes acid rain.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **vulcanising**: heating rubber with sulphur to link its chains
- **fungicide**: a substance that kills fungi such as mildew
- **electrolyte**: a liquid that conducts electricity by its ions
- **pickling**: cleaning a metal surface with acid before plating

- sulphur → sulphuric acid: the largest use
- SO₂: preserving fruit juice
- H₂SO₄: car batteries

Common mistake: “sulphur dioxide is used in car batteries” → “dilute sulphuric acid is used in car batteries”. The battery needs an electrolyte, which is the acid, not the gas.
