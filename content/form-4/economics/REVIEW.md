## Lesson 2: A Partnership

Prerequisites: 1 · Skill: n4-2 (Partnerships)

**Note** (draft)

> A partnership brings co-owners together to conduct a business and share its outcomes under an agreement. Partners may combine funds, skills and work, but must coordinate decisions. A partnership agreement can specify contributions, duties, profit sharing and handling disputes or departures. This introductory comparison uses an ordinary general partnership; limited-partnership forms differ. In that ordinary model, personal liability may be extensive. Do not assume that profit is shared equally or only according to capital unless the agreement says so.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to explain shared ownership and calculate a partner’s agreed profit share. Builds on: the sole proprietor’s finance and control limits. Leads to: companies and the distinction between owners’ liability and business debts.

**Learn: Understand the economics** (draft)

> A partnership brings co-owners together to conduct a business and share its outcomes under an agreement. Partners may combine funds, skills and work, but must coordinate decisions. A partnership agreement can specify contributions, duties, profit sharing and handling disputes or departures. This introductory comparison uses an ordinary general partnership; limited-partnership forms differ. In that ordinary model, personal liability may be extensive. Do not assume that profit is shared equally or only according to capital unless the agreement says so.

**Learn: Follow the local case** (draft)

> Bih supplies equipment and Ayuk provides funds to an imaginary Bamenda workshop. They agree on duties and a profit-sharing ratio. A disagreement about opening hours reveals a coordination cost. Their classroom agreement is an economic illustration, not a legally sufficient document or advice about the correct OHADA form.

**Learn: Reason and check** (draft)

> Read the agreement in the question. Separate capital contributions from profit allocations. Add ratio parts before dividing the distributable total, and check the partners’ shares sum to that total. Explain both the benefit of pooled resources and the possibility of conflict.

**Learn: key words and examples** (draft)

- **shared ownership**: co-owners jointly holding interests in the business
- **partnership agreement**: the arrangement stating the partners’ rights, contributions and responsibilities
- **pooled expertise**: combining different partners’ relevant skills
- **decision conflict**: disagreement between co-owners about business choices


Common mistake: “Every person paid by a partnership becomes a partner.” → “shared ownership: co-owners jointly holding interests in the business.”. Co-ownership is different from being an employee or supplier.

## Lesson 6: Capital structure of companies

Prerequisites: 3, 4 · Skill: n4-6 (Capital structure of companies)

**Note** (draft)

> Capital structure describes the financing mix, especially equity and longer-term debt. Equity includes owners’ funds and retained earnings; debt requires repayment under agreed terms. In the stated school share-capital model, authorised capital is the permitted nominal ceiling, issued capital the nominal value allocated, called capital the amount requested on issued shares, and paid-up capital the amount actually received. These are distinct from a share’s market price. Legal terminology and permitted payment schedules vary; the model does not announce an OHADA filing rule or minimum capital.

## Lesson 12: Nationalisation of industries

**Note** (draft)

## Lesson 13: Privatisation of industries

Prerequisites: 12 · Skill: n4-13 (Privatisation of industries)

**Note** (draft)

> Privatisation moves enterprise ownership from public to private interests, wholly or partly in the stated arrangement. It is distinct from liberalisation, which opens or changes competition rules, and commercialisation, which changes how an enterprise operates without necessarily selling it. Possible aims include finance, incentives or efficiency; concerns include access, market power, sale value and employment adjustment. Ownership change alone guarantees none of these outcomes. The lessons compare hypothetical policies rather than recommend a real transaction.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

## Lesson 14: Economies of scale

Prerequisites: 6, 11 · Skill: n4-14 (Economies of scale)

**Note** (draft)

> Economies of scale occur when larger output scale lowers long-run average cost as the firm can vary all inputs. Internal economies arise within the firm, such as specialisation, technical indivisibilities or purchasing advantages. External economies can arise from an industry’s expansion, such as specialised suppliers or a relevant skills pool. Total cost can rise while average cost falls. A purchasing discount is useful only if storage, quality and demand make the larger order worthwhile. Size does not guarantee a cost saving.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)

> By the end, you should be able to explain why larger scale can lower long-run average cost and calculate the saving per unit. Builds on: resources, finance and the cost of supplying output. Leads to: diseconomies of scale and the limits to expansion.

## Lesson 15: Diseconomies of scale

Prerequisites: 14 · Skill: n4-15 (Diseconomies of scale)

**Note** (draft)

> Diseconomies of scale occur when expansion raises long-run average cost. Communication delays, coordination difficulties and weak monitoring can offset benefits of size. External diseconomies can arise as industry expansion creates congestion or pressure on scarce local resources. A temporary input-price shock is not automatically an internal diseconomy caused by a firm’s scale. Economies and diseconomies may operate together; the net cost pattern matters. Higher total spending alone does not establish diseconomies.

**Learn: Your goal and connections** (draft)
